A fair race between a floor and a screen

For years one contract, the Bund future on German government bonds, traded in two places at once: by open outcry on LIFFE’s floor in London and on the screens of Germany’s DTB. The floor had most of the business, around 70%.

Then, nudged by German regulators, volume began to move to the screen; by October 1997 the two had about the same share. Same contract, same volume, two ways of trading: a natural experiment.

Over 30 days in October and November 1997, Frino, McInish and Toner (1998) found the screen’s spreads 5–10% narrower. On a spread of about one basis point (0.01%), or 25 marks, the gain per trade was tiny; for traders who would otherwise have used the floor, it came to DM 30–60 million a year.

Storms measured in minutes

Speed has its own risk. On 6 May 2010, around 2:45 pm, a single massive sell order in E-mini S&P 500 futures set off a fall of about 1,000 points in the Dow Jones, roughly a trillion in market value; 600 points came back within 30 minutes. Similar machine-speed slumps followed on 8 August 2011 and 24 August 2015.

Each reached Sydney, where the SPI’s daily volatility spiked. But these storms are measured in minutes and days, the crises in months and years. The paper judges the flash crash’s impact very short-lived, with little effect on liquidity over the longer term.

The authors raise one worry: local traders bore risk on the old floors when markets fell; with high-frequency traders in their place, markets may react more to sudden swings in the appetite for risk.
