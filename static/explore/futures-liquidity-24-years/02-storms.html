Meanwhile, two storms

Between the machines came two crises: the Global Financial Crisis of 2007–09, in which the S&P 500 fell 48%, and Covid-19 in 2020–21, in which it fell 34%.

In a storm, trading now gets dearer. Prices swing harder. Margin requirements, the cash a trader must post against losses, rise as prices fall and can force selling into the fall. Robert Webb, cited by the authors, argues that markets lean on traders willing to bear risk, whose capital and appetite for it may not last a storm. In Covid the average cost of trading corporate bonds rose almost threefold, to 0.9%.

In the SPI the storms are plain to see: its volatility towers in 2008–09 and in 2020, and the depth at the best prices fell sharply in 2008 and again in 2020.

Place your bet: Covid against the screens

To weigh machines and storms on one scale, the authors give every year five switches: on from the arrival of screens, of high-frequency trading and of co-location, and on during the GFC (2007–09) and Covid (2020–21). A regression on 8,661,139 one-minute snapshots of the SPI then estimates how far the average spread shifts when each switch turns on.

The switch for screens comes out at 2.4. How big is the Covid switch next to that 2.4?

What to take away

Machines or storms? For one Australian futures contract over a quarter century, the paper’s answer is the storms, by a wide margin.

  1. Two of the three technology changes, each studied around its own date, narrowed the SPI’s spread a little: about 15% off the floor, about 2.5% with co-location. “Small but highly significant”, in the authors’ words.
  2. In the paper’s regression the GFC switch is about four times each technology switch and the Covid switch about twenty times. Liquidity, the authors conclude, is “captive to outlier events”.
  3. They read this as consistent with the hypothesis that economic uncertainty is, by far, what moves liquidity.

The caveat. In the authors’ words, Table 1 is “a simple OLS regression”, run “to provide an indication”. Its five switches are on or off for whole years, three of them staying on once they arrive, so it compares average spreads between blocks of years; together the switches explain about 4% of the minute-to-minute variation.

Left open: high-frequency traders replaced the floor’s locals, who once bore risk in a crash. In the next storm, do the machines stand or step back?

One to try, if you skipped it: on the staircase, which hole is deeper, 2019 without its three machines or 2008 without its storm?
