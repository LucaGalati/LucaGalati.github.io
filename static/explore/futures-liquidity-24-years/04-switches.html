Reading a simple regression

The authors call Table 1 “a simple OLS regression”, run “to provide an indication” of how technology compares with the crises. Six things that help in reading it.

  • Significant is not bigWith 8,661,139 one-minute observations even a small difference can pass the 0.001 test, which says a difference is unlikely to be chance, not that it is large. The authors draw the same line: in their abstract, technology’s influence is “statistically significant but almost imperceptible”.
  • Blocks of yearsEach switch is on or off for whole years, so the five split 1996–2021 into six blocks, and the regression’s line runs through each block’s average. Each coefficient is a step between two blocks: screens, for instance, compare the minutes of 2000–06 and 2010 with those of 1996–99.
  • Four percentTogether the switches account for 3.9% of the variation in one-minute spreads (R² of 0.039; residual standard error 34.77). They track the average from block to block, while single minutes scatter widely around it.
  • One contract, one marketSPI futures in Sydney, the authors’ case study. The contract’s underlying index changed in May 2000, and the paper drops “the delivery contract in 2016” after noting anomalies in the data.
  • Two spreadsThe spread in Table 1 is the gap between ask and bid (Appendix A, eq. 4). The paper also computes a percentage spread, the gap divided by the midpoint, which allows for the level of the index (Figure 6).
  • What comes nextThe authors suggest other contracts and markets, longer periods, tests of what high-frequency and algorithmic trading do to liquidity in crises, and of how support from the authorities affects it.
