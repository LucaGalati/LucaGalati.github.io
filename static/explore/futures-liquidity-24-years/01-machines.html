What changed the cost of trading more: the machines, or the storms?

The SPI is a futures contract on the Australian share index: a deal made at today’s price and settled on the index’s value at a later date. In 1996 it still traded by people in coloured jackets, shouting and signalling across the floor of the Sydney Futures Exchange; by 2021, between computers, some parked right beside the exchange’s own. Meanwhile the money traded in it grew more than ninefold, from A$134 billion in 1998 to A$1,251 billion in 2021.

Every new machine promised cheaper trading. The same years brought two storms: the Global Financial Crisis and Covid-19.

Which did more to what it costs to trade: three revolutions in technology, or two crises?

Liquidity is the price of now

Trade the SPI and you meet an order book. At the front of the queue sit the best price anyone will pay right now, the bid, and the best price anyone will sell at right now, the ask. To trade at once, you cross the gap.

The paper sums the book up in two numbers. The spread is the gap between the best ask and the best bid, in index points, the units the index itself is quoted in. The depth is how many contracts wait at those best prices. An order that fits inside the depth pays half the spread above the midpoint, the price halfway between bid and ask; a bigger one climbs to worse prices.

Place your bet: what did the screens do to the spread?

On the trading floor, the SPI’s spread averaged about 1.4 index points. In 1999 the Sydney Futures Exchange closed the floor and moved the contract onto screens.

Deck 1 · The machines · 4 of 4

Each machine shaved a little

Screens let software trade. From the late 1990s to about 2007 the average SPI trade halved in size while the number of trades climbed steeply, which the authors read as a sign of algorithms slicing big orders into small ones. High-frequency trading, computers sending and cancelling orders in fractions of a second, the paper dates to 2010 and cites no before-and-after measure for it. In February 2012 the exchange let traders put their computers beside its own; message traffic rose and the spread narrowed by about 2.5%.

On one ruler the pattern is plain: each measured change shaved a little off the price of now. The authors call such gains “small but highly significant”. But the same quarter century held two storms.
