An explorable explanation · Galati, Webb & Webb

You could trade. You could not leave.

On the morning of 8 November 2022, FTX, which that year handled between 5% and 9% of all crypto trading, stopped letting most customers withdraw their coins. Its trading screen stayed on: until 03:28 UTC on 12 November, anyone with a balance could buy and sell as before. Next door, Binance, the largest exchange, still let its customers withdraw.

Exchanges have closed in crises before; New York’s stock exchange shut for over four months in 1914. This was the reverse: the market open, the exit shut. The paper calls the balances caught inside stranded assets.

What would you do with money you can rearrange but not take home? And what happens to prices that can no longer be checked against the rest of the world?

Into Tether, or out altogether

Why rearrange money you cannot take out? The freeze said two things at once: your coins are locked, and the exchange may be going under. What you hold on the day it fails may decide what you get back.

The trade data record which side started each trade. For every coin traded against Tether, a stablecoin built to stay worth one dollar, selling pressure rose: more trades were started by sellers than by buyers. Coins were also sold for dollars, which the authors read as attempts to leave crypto altogether. They call the pattern a flight to safety.

Selling pressure: Figures 4a and 4b (pp. 11–13), read for direction only; stablecoin turnover: Figure 3. Inside FTX, a dollar coin was not worth a dollar

A stablecoin has one job: to be worth one dollar. Inside the frozen exchange two of them lost it. Tether, the coin traders were selling into, changed hands at around $1.20. DAI, a smaller stablecoin that traders were selling hard, at one point traded about 30% below its dollar.

Normally someone would buy Tether elsewhere for a dollar, bring it in and sell it here for $1.20, again and again, until the gap closed. Why did nobody?

Tether at around $1.20: p. 2. DAI about 30% below its $1 peg: p. 11; the $0.70 on the scale is computed from that. Heavier selling of DAI than of Tether: Figure 4a and p. 11.

One coin, two prices

Normally a coin costs about the same on every exchange, because traders keep it so. If it is cheaper on one, they buy it there, move it, and sell it where it is dearer, until the gap closes.

Try it on a toy coin listed on both exchanges. Trade on FTX a few times with withdrawals open; then freeze them and trade again.

Frozen withdrawals do not stop anyone trading. They stop the trip between exchanges: whoever tried to close a gap would end with money stuck inside FTX. So nobody does, and the price inside drifts wherever the stranded traders push it.

Toy: price index 100, each trade on FTX moves its price by 3%. The arbitrage logic is the textbook one, not a test in the paper; the paper notes that FTX customers could not move assets to Binance (p. 14).

Bitcoin held its price. BNB did not.

On the real exchange, prices stayed close to the market for about a day after the freeze, then began to drift; by 11 November most coins in the study had drifted far from it.

Bitcoin, the first and most popular coin, kept its price: nobody inside paid extra for it. The puzzle is BNB, the token of Binance, FTX’s rival, which had just walked away from buying it. Inside the frozen exchange it traded at several times its market price. The authors pass the reason on to future research.
