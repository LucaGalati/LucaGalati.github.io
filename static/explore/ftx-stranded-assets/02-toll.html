Trading right now has a price

On an exchange a coin has no single price. Some traders post the most they will pay, the bid; others the least they will sell for, the ask. Whoever wants to trade at once sells at the bid or buys at the ask. The gap between the two, the spread, is the toll for not waiting; halfway across it sits the midpoint, the fairest single price.

How many coins are waiting at those prices is the market’s depth. When it is thin, a larger order has to reach further down the queue, at worse prices.

Why a market maker widens the gap

Someone has to post those bids and asks: a market maker, or in the paper’s words a liquidity provider, who earns the spread and carries the risk of whatever they end up holding. Be one, on a toy exchange where stranded holders mostly want to sell.

With the exit open, you pass the coins you buy on to another exchange each hour. With it shut, they pile up in your account while the price slides. Find the narrowest spread that does not lose money, first with the exit open, then shut.

A toy in the spirit of the paper’s explanation (p. 6): in a funding crisis, liquidity providers face more risk and one-sided selling, and widen their quotes to cover it.

Toy, every number illustrative: 12 sellers and 4 buyers an hour for 12 hours; a coin priced 100 that falls steadily by the amount you choose; trades thin to nothing at a 3% half-spread; passing a coin on costs 0.1% when the exit is open. Traders paid more; liquidity providers kept more

The effective spread is what a trade paid, measured from the midpoint. The realized spread is what the provider kept: the effective spread minus the price impact, how far the midpoint moved in the trade’s direction a moment later.

Kept can exceed paid. On FTX, Table 2’s price impact is negative in both windows, and subtracting a negative number adds to what the provider keeps.

On FTX both spreads rose sharply after the freeze; on Binance far less. The paper reads this as providers earning more for the risks of a funding crisis.

What to take away

For four days FTX was a market you could trade in but not leave.

  1. Trading did not stop: about twice as many trades in Bitcoin, Ether and BNB as in the four days before, nearly three times as many in FTT, though most coins peaked on the first day.
  2. Trading inside got far dearer: Bitcoin’s spread grew about 24 times, against 2.7 on Binance. The paper’s explanation: providers facing more risk and one-sided selling widen their quotes.
  3. With no way to carry coins out and sell them elsewhere, nothing pulled prices inside back to the market: Bitcoin held its price, BNB went over 400% above it.
  4. Traders appear to have run toward safety, selling into Tether or dollars; that motive is the paper’s reading.

The evidence compares four-day averages before and after, one measure at a time, around a single event; Binance, the yardstick, was touched by the shock too.

A question the authors pass on to future research: why would anyone pay several times the market price for BNB, the token of FTX’s rival, inside an exchange they could not leave?
