When a war starts next door, what happens to the cost of trading?

At 03:53 UTC on 24 February 2022, missiles struck Kyiv and Kharkiv. The stock exchanges nearest the fighting were closed for the night. They would open a few hours later.

An earlier study found that share prices of firms near the border fell further than those of firms far away. This paper looks underneath the prices, at what it cost to buy or sell a share at a moment like that. That cost is the spread: the gap between the best price you can buy at and the best you can sell at, paid by anyone who needs to trade right now.

In the World Wars, exchanges near the fighting often simply shut. In 2022 they opened as usual. So did the market keep working that morning, and did a war next door make trading dearer in Warsaw than in New York?

The shock arrived while the nearest markets were shut

By the open, four hours of escalating news had piled up with nowhere to trade it; the opening auction had to absorb it all at once. For the study that is a gift: the same firms on the same exchanges, a few hours apart, with the shock pinned to the minute.

Each firm’s spread is measured against its own normal: its average in the same trading hour on five ordinary days a fortnight earlier, counted in units of its usual day-to-day wobble, the standard deviation (sd). On an ordinary day the number sits within about ±1.

Two rings around Ukraine

The paper’s first measure of exposure is blunt, and the authors know it. A firm is a first-degree neighbour if its headquarters is in a country that borders Ukraine, a second-degree neighbour if its country borders one of those. Everyone else is distant. Of the 2,747 firms in the main sample, 448 sit in the two rings, about 15% of the observations.

Borders are a crude ruler. The authors therefore also measure each headquarters’ distance to Ukraine in kilometres, so they can test slope against cliff. First, though: how much wider did spreads get?
