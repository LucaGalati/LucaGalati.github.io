About ten basis points more per trade

The regression version adds a fixed effect for every firm and for every day, and controls for size, price level and quoting activity. After the invasion, quoted spreads of firms in countries bordering Ukraine widened about 9–12 basis points (hundredths of a percent) more than those of similar distant firms, roughly a sixth of the sample’s pre-war average of about 60 bp. Effective spreads widened about 9–10 bp more.

And yet they traded more

If trading got dearer, you might expect less of it. The opposite happened. Firms in bordering countries are thin markets to begin with: before the war a typical one traded about $0.5 million of shares a day, against about $4.5 million for the rest. After the invasion their trading rose faster than everyone else’s.

Raw changes in logs, twenty days after against twenty before (Table 3). Their gaps, 0.22 and 0.17, tell the same story as the regression.

Relative to otherwise similar distant firms, dollar volume in bordering-country firms rose about 22–25% more and the number of trades about 13–19% more (Table 6). Wider spreads and heavier trading at once: investors kept moving war risk around and paid up to do it, “a functioning albeit deteriorating market”, in the paper’s words. Trading went on, at a higher price.

Typical daily dollar volume from Table 3’s mean log volume before the invasion: e13.12 ≈ $0.5 million for bordering firms, e15.33 ≈ $4.5 million for the rest. Prices moved. That is not why spreads widened.

The obvious rival story: firms near the war simply lost more value, and spreads follow prices. So the authors swap geography for each firm’s own share-price return on 24 February. Here is the extra quoted spread each kind of exposure goes with:

Returns do line up with trading: with controls, the firms that fell most were traded most. Intraday volatility, tried the same way, adds little (Tables 10 and 11). Prices say what a firm is worth; spreads say what it costs to trade it right now. Near the war, spreads rose more than price falls can explain.

What to take away

For the twenty trading days after the invasion, a firm in a country bordering Ukraine paid about 9–12 bp more to trade than a similar firm far away.

  1. Spreads near Ukraine gave no sign that dealers saw the timing coming; they jumped at the first open after the strikes.
  2. Who paid followed geography, not how far a stock fell. The paper reads this as dealers fearing better-informed counterparties and riskier inventory near the war: consistent with the evidence, not tested directly.
  3. Markets kept trading, at a higher cost, exactly when investors needed to rebalance.

The jump at the open rests on 54 nearby firms and is significant only at the 10% level; inside the bordering countries, extra closeness adds little.

The paper stops at listed equities and a forty-day window. Bonds, derivatives, bank balance sheets and other wars are open questions, as is whether distance to the fighting belongs in a regulator’s stress test.

A question to take with you: where does Vienna sit on the curve, and does that match the paper’s separate estimate for the second ring, about 3.5–4 bp (Table 5)?
