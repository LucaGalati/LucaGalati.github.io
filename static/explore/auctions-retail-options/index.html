Deck 1 of 4 · The order nobody bought

An explorable explanation · working paper, 2026

Auctions for Retail-Sized Options

Luca Galati, Terrence Hendershott, Saad Ali Khan and Ryan Riordan

Individual investors place about 62 percent of U.S. listed-option volume, and their brokers sell those orders: in 2021, trading firms called wholesalers paid $2.4 billion for them. The wholesaler fills the order against its own account, in part through auctions on an exchange in which rivals may offer a better price.

What we know about those auctions comes from orders a wholesaler had already bought. Does the auction help the investor on its own?

One market can tell. SPX options, on the S&P 500 index itself, trade on one exchange, Cboe, and nobody pays brokers for their orders. On February 22, 2021, Cboe opened its auction to SPX orders of ten contracts or fewer.

Does exposing a small order that nobody bought to an auction make it cheaper to execute? And if it does, who pays?

Start with the auction itself

The mechanism

What an auction does to a price

The firm holding the customer’s order offers to take the other side at a price it names. While the exchange exposes the order, anyone else may offer a better price for part or all of it. If someone does, the firm may match that price and keep 40 or 50 percent of the order; the rest goes to the best bidders.

Try it. The customer is buying ten contracts, the most the SPX auction takes. Prices move in ten-cent ticks.

Why the tick matters

Where SPX trades go

Many trades, little money

How much of SPX goes through this auction? Over the paper’s sample it took 11.27 percent of SPX trades but only 1.04 percent of the dollars: many small orders. The trading floor is the mirror image, with 2.77 percent of the trades and 62.64 percent of the dollars.

In SPY, where wholesalers buy the orders and any size may enter, the auction takes 23 percent of trades.

The catch

Auction trades are not book trades

Auction trades pay less: 6.28 cents against 8.99 in the book. Case closed? Look at the rest of the table.

The auction’s trades are smaller, closer to expiry and more exposed to the index, and they face wider quotes. Orders are routed on the very characteristics that set their cost, so the comparison mixes the mechanism with the orders it attracts.

The paper’s answer: for each auction trade, find its twin, a book trade of the same size and risk from the two weeks before the auction existed, when no order could choose it.
