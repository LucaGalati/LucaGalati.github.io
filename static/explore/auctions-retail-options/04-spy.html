With and without a buyer

Two auctions on one index

Does an auction behave differently when nobody bought the order?

SPY options track one tenth of the same index on sixteen exchanges. A wholesaler buys the order from the broker, routes it to an exchange where it holds the market-maker appointment and often starts the auction itself. In auctions on stock options, where wholesalers also buy the orders, fewer than one in ten draws a second bidder at the winning price.

In SPX nobody bought the order and there is no second venue. A customer limit order resting at the best quote takes priority over the initiator’s contra order at the same price.

The paper pairs each SPX auction trade from February 22 to December 31, 2021 with its nearest SPY auction trade: 1.18 million single-leg and 0.82 million complex matched observations.

Pick a ruler

Which auction is cheaper?

An SPX option on an index at 4,405 and an SPY option on a fund at 440 cannot be compared in cents: the matched SPX auction trade pays 6.49 cents, its SPY twin 0.66. Change the ruler and the comparison changes.

Per unit of the underlying the two cost the same. But the SPX auction starts from a quote about 1.7 times as wide per unit of the underlying, and closes far more of it. Hold the quote fixed and SPX is cheaper.

Competition you can count

More hands in the fill

The options tape does not say who responded to an auction. It does record each print, and a fill split among several liquidity providers leaves several prints. An SPX fill leaves 0.33 to 0.41 more prints than its SPY twin for single-leg orders and 0.46 to 0.50 more for complex ones: about one extra print for every two or three fills.

That is consistent with more providers competing for each order, in a market where nobody bought the order from the broker.

Two objections

Exercise style and tick size

Early exercise (SPY options are American, SPX options European) or the coarser SPX tick could explain the gap. So the paper repeats the comparison on in- and at-the-money options, where early exercise matters, and on options priced at $3 or more, where the two ticks are equal relative to the underlying.

The gap survives both cuts: the SPX trade still executes much closer to the midpoint and leaves more prints.

The tick

What the tick does

When the bid and the ask are one tick apart, no price lies between them, and an auction can only execute at the quote. A one-tick quote cuts the odds of auction execution to about a fifth, in both markets.

In the weeks around the switch, 62 percent of SPY trades faced a one-tick quote, against 15 percent of SPX trades. SPY is tick-bound four times as often, which should hold its auction share down. It still takes twice the SPX share: the tick works against that gap, not for it.

The paper reads the smaller SPX share as consistent with two other facts: nobody is paid to route an SPX order into the auction, and nothing above ten contracts may enter.

What the auction is, on its own

An auction without a buyer

The SEC proposed in 2022, and withdrew in 2025, a rule to expose individual investors’ equity orders to auctions like these, on evidence from markets where wholesalers buy the orders. SPX shows the auction on its own:

  • Single-leg orders: a third to a half cheaper, and the liquidity provider pays.
  • Complex orders: no change; the book already fills them deep inside the quote.
  • Against SPY: closer to the midpoint, more providers per fill, but fewer orders reach it.
  • One-tick quotes: nothing to improve, for 62 percent of SPY trades and 15 percent of SPX.

Whether wholesalers would keep paying for orders that must be exposed one by one is, the authors write, an open question.
