The model’s memory

The model behind Table 1, a scalar BEKK, revises its estimate of how two coins move together every minute. Each new estimate blends three things:

estimatenow = (1 − a − b) × long-run average + a × (surpriseA × surpriseB) + b × estimatea minute ago

A coin’s surprise is simply its minute’s move, since the model expects zero on average; two moves in the same direction make a positive product. a sets how hard the estimate reacts to a fresh joint surprise, b how long it remembers. While a + b stays below 1, every jolt fades back towards the long-run average. “Scalar” means one a and one b serve every pair of coins. Table 1 averages a line like this one over each week.

What a t-statistic weighs

Every change in Table 1 comes with a t-statistic. It sets the gap between two averages against how uncertain those averages are: the gap divided by its standard error. The standard error grows with the spread of the observations and shrinks as more of them are averaged, so the same gap earns a larger t from more data.

The further t lies from zero, the harder the gap is to put down to chance: beyond 2.58 it clears the 1% level (**), beyond 3.29 the 0.1% level (***). The authors chose the strict 0.1% level to keep false alarms rare in high-frequency data (Section 2.2).
