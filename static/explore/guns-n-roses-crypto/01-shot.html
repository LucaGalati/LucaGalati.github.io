A shot on a Saturday evening. Which way would crypto jump?

At 22:11 UTC on 13 July 2024, a shot was fired at Donald Trump at a campaign rally in Pennsylvania. He survived. It was a Saturday: stock exchanges were shut for the weekend, but crypto markets never close.

All year, Trump’s campaign had courted crypto, setting itself against rivals who wanted closer oversight. So the news carried two messages at once. One was fear: sudden doubt about who would lead and which policies would follow. The other, once he was known to have survived, was hope: a pro-crypto agenda that now looked more durable.

Which message would the market hear? The authors ask two things: which way ten very different tokens moved, and whether they moved more tightly together.

An explorable explanation of Galati and Russo (2026). Time of the event: Section 3.1 and the caption of Fig. 1. The two messages are the paper’s two channels (p. 2); its three questions, p. 2. Crypto trades around the clock (footnote 4); 13 July 2024 was a Saturday.

Ten tokens, each measured against its own normal

The authors follow ten of the largest tokens minute by minute, from a week before the shot to a week after.

Each token is measured against its own normal: its average minute return from 1 June to 5 July, since crypto has no closing price and no market index to compare with. Whatever a token does beyond that normal is its abnormal return. Added up minute after minute, these give its cumulative abnormal return (CAR): a line that climbs while the token runs ahead of its usual pace.

The authors’ title promises roses: if the market heard hope, the gains should bloom. Take each token’s CAR line and work out its average height over the week before the shot, then over the week after. The paper’s measure is the difference, after minus before.

Deck 1 · The shot · 5 of 5

Ten designs, one direction

The ten tokens differ in what they are for, how they are built and who holds them. Had each been repriced only on its own news, some would have gained and some lost. All ten gained.

The paper reads this as an information cascade (Tse and Hackard, 2006): when traders copy what the market does instead of weighing news about each token, abnormal returns pile up the same way everywhere. The pattern is consistent with such herding; prices alone cannot show the herd itself.

The paper reads the sizes too. The largest gains went to narrative-driven tokens such as Dogecoin, Shiba Inu and XRP, the most muted, though still significant, to protocol-oriented Polkadot and Avalanche: in its reading, the intensity of speculative buying within the cascade, rather than hedging or safe-haven flows.
