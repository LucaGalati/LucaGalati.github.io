The counterfactual

Did the posted fee make Ethereum cheaper? Before-and-after cannot say: fees move with the coin's price, with congestion, with whatever else happened that summer.

So the paper adds a control: Bitcoin. It runs the same proof-of-work design, in which miners compete with computing power to build each block; its fees were of the same order; and it left its auction alone. Is that a fair comparison? The reform gets credit only for the part of Ethereum's move that Bitcoin did not share.

Side trip · from The counterfactual

Is Bitcoin a fair control?

The paper's case (p. 4): Bitcoin shares Ethereum's original proof-of-work design, the two are the most widely used chains, their fees were of the same order, and Bitcoin changed neither its auction nor its block rules during the window.

It also names the doubts. Bitcoin's own supply of block space moved as its miners recovered from the mid-2021 Chinese mining ban, so every specification controls for block difficulty and block size. Ethereum runs applications and Bitcoin mostly payments, so the controls include contract activity, transaction size and prices. Before the switch, the two chains' fees track each other (Figure 1), and placebo dates before the event show no comparable jumps.

The hardest test removes the reliance on one control: four smaller chains join Bitcoin. Some results survive; some do not.

Why did it get dearer?

Look at what a transaction paid. Before the reform the whole fee was a bid: $8.90 on average, all of it to the miner. After it, the tip, the part users still bid for priority, averaged $8.37: about what the whole fee used to be. On top of it came the base fee, $22.38, burned.

The paper's reading: users kept bidding for priority much as before and paid a burned base fee on top (p. 7). It does not claim to have isolated this channel.

In raw dollars the tip barely moved. In the regressions, which also hold ether's price and transaction size fixed, tips fell 32 to 39 per cent relative to Bitcoin (Table 4): the "about a third" of the paper's abstract.

The promise kept

The reform promised predictability, and inside the block it delivered some. The paper's fee volatility, the logarithm of the highest fee over the lowest in each block, fell by about 12 per cent relative to Bitcoin.

Day to day, the typical change in Ethereum's average fee fell by a quarter while Bitcoin's stayed flat, though that estimate is too noisy to be significant in a 60-day window.

The gap in dollars widened

Take the same two transactions, the dearest and the cheapest in a block, and subtract. The paper calls the difference dispersion. On Ethereum it went from $127 to $341, up 105 to 112 per cent relative to Bitcoin. Divided by the number of transactions in the block it becomes the gap per transaction, which the paper calls fee inflation: $1.15 to $3.71, up 134 to 164 per cent.

Two cautions. Transactions that use more computation cost more, so not all of a dollar gap is unequal pricing (pp. 17–18). And Bitcoin's in-block gap was larger still: $430 before, $392 after. Didn't another study find the gap fell?

Side trip · from The gap in dollars

Didn't another study find the gap fell?

Liu et al. (2022) studied the same reform on Ethereum alone. They found average gas prices essentially unchanged, waits shorter and fees easier to estimate, and a dispersion measure that fell as the new transaction format spread. The paper reconciles the two findings (p. 30): different objects, horizons and comparisons.

Read together: in the middle of the distribution, price differences per unit of gas narrowed as adoption spread; at the top, the dollar cost of priority, and the average dollar cost of settling, rose sharply. Nor is that a currency artifact: median gas prices rose from about 60 gwei at activation to about 180 gwei in September and October 2021 (Karaivanov and Zarifian 2025).

Narrower ratio, wider gap

Take the cheapest and the dearest transaction in a block. The paper's fee volatility is the logarithm of their ratio; its dispersion is their difference in dollars.

Slide a common floor under both, which is what a base fee does: the ratio shrinks and the gap does not move. Scale both up, which is what tripling fees does: the gap widens and the ratio does not move. Do both and you have the paper's pattern: lower volatility, higher dispersion.

Two views of one block. Together they say the fee distribution moved up and tightened at the bottom, while the contest for priority at the top went on.

Who got the money

Now the miner's side. Before the reform the miner kept the whole fee, $8.90, plus a protocol subsidy worth $33.69 per transaction. After it, the miner kept the tip, $8.37; the base fee went to nobody; and the subsidy per transaction had risen to $99.36.

The subsidy is fixed per block, 2 ETH at the time, so per transaction it rises when blocks carry fewer transactions, and in dollars when ether's price rises, which the regressions hold fixed. Net of Bitcoin, the subsidy per transaction rose 103 to 112 per cent, and the miner's total payoff per transaction 72 to 77 per cent.

So users paid more, and the extra was burned. Miners still earned more per transaction, but not from users: the fixed subsidy was shared by fewer transactions. Per transaction is not per day; the paper notes this does not mean miners' total income rose (p. 31).

Deck 2 · 7 of 8

Relative to Bitcoin, the network shrank

Elastic blocks were meant to let more through. Relative to Bitcoin, less did: about a fifth (21–22%) fewer confirmed transactions, 38 to 40 per cent less value settled, and 46 per cent fewer zero-value transactions, the contract calls that make Ethereum more than a payment rail. The unconfirmed queue shrank 43 to 44 per cent.

In raw terms Ethereum's activity barely moved; Bitcoin's surged, and the estimates measure the gap. Congestion fell alongside activity, and plausibly because of it.

Why would less get through? One reading the paper offers but cannot test: with the base fee burned, one more low-tip transaction earns a miner almost nothing. (That emptier blocks raise the subsidy per transaction is arithmetic, not a motive.)

What it cost in welfare

value transferred−fees paid+what miners earn

The paper sums this over every confirmed transaction in a day, following Basu, Easley, O'Hara and Sirer (2023). It is a settlement proxy: value transferred is an upper bound on what a transaction is worth, and a zero-value contract call counts as minus its fee. So the paper reads the contrast with Bitcoin, not the levels.

Ethereum's average daily welfare rose by $6.4 billion (from $14.6 to $21.0 billion a day) and Bitcoin's by $60.2 billion a day. That is a gap of $54 billion a day in raw means, and $80 to 84 billion a day once coin prices and block size are controlled for. Most of the gap is Bitcoin's surge: its settled value rose about 70 per cent in the window (Table 2).

How robust is it?
  • Raw means · −$53.8 bn a day (Table 2)
  • With controls · −$83.6 bn a day (coin price, block size) and −$80.2 bn (adding coin volatility and block difficulty), significant at 1%, also when errors may be correlated from day to day (White, Newey-West and Driscoll-Kraay errors; Tables 9, A5)
  • Day fixed effects · −$59.6 to −67.9 bn a day (two-way fixed effects, errors clustered by chain and day)
  • Five control chains · −$57.9 to −57.3 bn a day (Table A4)
  • Counting the burn · a second measure adds the burned base fee back; it is nearly identical, because the burn is small next to the value settled
