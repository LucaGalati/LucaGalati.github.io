Ethereum settles about a quarter of what Visa settles in a year. The volume is there; the price is not. Visa posts its fees in advance. A blockchain user has to win an auction for space in the next block, at a clearing price nobody knows at the moment they submit.

And that bill is what users paid after the reform designed to fix the auction. To see what the reform changed, start with the thing being sold.

Space in a block is scarce

A block has a maximum size, and it cannot be expanded the way a payment processor adds servers. Users attach a fee to each transaction. Miners, who build the blocks, pick which transactions go in and keep the fees, along with a subsidy the protocol pays them for each block.

Unlike a stock exchange's order book, a blockchain does not serve you in order of arrival. In the seconds between two blocks, the mechanism itself decides who gets in. Some rationing rule is unavoidable. The question is not whether to price priority, but how.

The fix: a posted fee

On 5 August 2021 Ethereum activated EIP-1559. Three rules replaced the auction:

  • One price for everyone. Each transaction pays the same base fee, set by the protocol: up after a block more than half full, down after one less than half full. It is burned: destroyed, paid to nobody.
  • A tip. An optional extra, the only part the miner receives.
  • Elastic blocks. A block can stretch from 15 to 30 million gas when demand spikes.

Gas measures computation: every step a transaction asks of the network costs gas, and fees are quoted per unit of gas in gwei, a billionth of an ether.

Side trip · from The fix: a posted fee

Why burn the fee?

Burning takes the base fee out of circulation. Nobody receives it: it is subtracted from the supply of ether, offsetting the new ether issued to miners, and in busy periods more is burned than issued (Appendix C). The benefit is spread over everyone who holds ether (p. 5). The paper cites Félez-Viñas et al. (2021): in the months after the reform, burning cut the net issuance of new ether by roughly half.

Why not hand it to the miner? Because a miner who never receives the base fee has nothing to gain from steering it, and nothing to share with users in a side deal. The paper cites Roughgarden (2024): the design is incentive-compatible for short-sighted miners and robust to off-chain agreements.

Miners objected, since part of their income was gone; they kept the tips and the 2-ETH block subsidy (Appendix C).

The thermostat

Think of the base fee as a thermostat. Demand has just risen to 18 transactions a block, above the target of 15, so the fee climbs until just enough people are willing to pay. Drop demand below 15 and it falls. Press burst: the block stretches toward 30 to absorb the spike, and the fee rises a step at a time instead of jumping.

Then notice what the thermostat cannot do. Keep demand high and the fee stays high. A posted price removes the guesswork; it does not create space. Roughgarden (2020) argues that no fee mechanism can substantially lower average fees when capacity is the constraint.
