Nine digits, one law

Take the net incomes in a stack of company accounts and look only at their first digits. You might expect each of the nine about equally often. Instead, about 30% start with a 1 and fewer than 5% with a 9.

This is Benford’s law. It holds for numbers that range over many sizes and are built from other numbers, as a net income is: revenues, less costs, less taxes. Accounting data fit it well.

Here is why that helps. A first digit cannot show a loss turned into a profit, but numbers someone has adjusted often stray from the law, so the paper takes the quality of the digits as a stand-in for steering. And a good year, which scales results up, changes how many are losses but, under mild conditions, not their first digits.

Rounding up leaves a mark

Which adjustments leave a mark? The best known is rounding up: a company that made €99,873 may prefer to report €100,000, and accounting research has long found managers doing just that. Look at the first digit: a 9 becomes a 1.

Try it on 2,320 invented results that follow the law. The readout scores the gap with the paper’s χ² test; its p-value is the chance of a gap at least this large if nobody had touched the numbers. Below 0.10 is a warning, below 0.01 strong evidence.

Remember this shape: more 1s, fewer 9s. You will soon test real accounts for it. The toy cannot say how much rounding real data would hide.

Toy: 2,320 invented results between €100 and €10 million, as many as the paper has per period for its 100% owned companies; “just short” means within 5% below a power of ten. χ²: the paper’s equation (2), each squared gap between observed and expected share divided by the law’s share, summed, times the number of results; the toy’s p-value uses 8 degrees of freedom. The €99,873 example and the rounding reading: p. 352, 355, after Carslaw (1988) and Thomas (1989).

Before the rule, every group passes

The paper sorts the companies by the stake of their largest municipal owner and tests each group twice: before the rule, 2010–2014, and under it, 2015–2019.

Besides χ² it runs a second test, on the excess mean absolute deviation (MAD): the average gap between the observed shares and the law, less the gap that chance alone leaves with that many results, so that a large group does not fail on trifles.

Before the rule, every group passes both tests. That clean start matters: it says the data suit the method, and that whatever shows up later was not there before.
