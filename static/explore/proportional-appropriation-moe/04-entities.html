The city’s companies

Much of what an Italian town provides is run not by the town hall but by companies it owns, in whole or in part. The paper calls them municipally owned entities. With the public-management reforms of past decades, cities handed most of the services residents use to companies like these.

They add up: the state’s stakes in companies were worth nearly a tenth of Italy’s GDP by 2019, and in 2020 about two thirds of the active companies with a public stake were owned directly by local governments.

Because these services cannot simply stop, a company that keeps losing money ends up being rescued by its owner. That used to be the only link between a company’s losses and the city’s money: late, and all at once.

Owner, client and appointer

  • Ownerthe city is usually the largest shareholder.
  • Clientit is often the company’s main customer too, paying for the service under a contract, so its decisions weigh heavily on the company’s finances.
  • Appointerthe mayor can name and remove the directors, and when the city owns more than half, does so with very wide discretion. Below 50% the city has no formal say in how the company is run.

Many directors come from politics, and because these companies employ many people their results are politically sensitive. A mayor serves five years, a board three, so every mayor gets to renew at least one board: a director who wants another term needs the favour of the mayor in office. The paper calls for shielding managers from politics.
