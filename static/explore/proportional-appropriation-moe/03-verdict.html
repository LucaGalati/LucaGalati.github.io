What a before-and-after design can show

Four questions to ask of any comparison of digits before and under a rule, with what the paper does or says about each.

The questionWhat the paper does or says
Is there an untouched comparison group?No. The paper acknowledges that a comparison group would ideally be untouched by the rule (note 4). Companies with a city stake of 50% or less serve as its comparison, although they too fall under the rule (p. 349); private firms operate, are regulated and report too differently to compare (note 4). The evidence rests on the difference across stakes.
Were 2015–2019 simply better years?A count of losses moves with the economy (p. 354). First digits should not: a business cycle that scales results up or down leaves Benford’s law in place, under mild conditions (note 5).
Do odd digits show intent?The test reads the quality of the numbers. It cannot say whether results were pushed up or down, it cannot prove fraud, and processing errors remain a possible explanation (p. 358).
Do other windows and samples agree?The paper reports qualitatively similar results with windows of three and four years around the rule, and finds that utility companies do not drive its findings (p. 353, 357).

What to take away

After the rule, the first digits reported by companies wholly owned by Italian cities departed from Benford’s law, those of majority-owned companies at the 10% level, those of minority-owned companies not at all. The paper reads this as a sign, not a proof, of steered results.

  1. From the 2015 accounts, a loss at a city-owned company takes money out of the city’s next budget in proportion to its stake. The paper argues this gives mayor and managers a shared reason to make results look healthier.
  2. Before the rule every group’s first digits follow Benford’s law, so the departures appear only after it. They are larger the larger the city’s stake, a pattern the paper reads as consistent with its second prediction.
  3. The test measures the quality of the numbers, not intent: it cannot tell whether results were pushed up or down, and it cannot prove fraud.

The limits the paper names: there is no untouched comparison group (note 4), and processing errors remain a possible explanation (p. 358).

Open questions the authors raise: did the rule cut the losses it targeted and save the money it meant to save? Do politicians take part in the steering? Would other countries with many publicly owned companies see the same?

A question to take with you: if every owner had to set aside the same amount whatever its stake, which of the paper’s two predictions would still make sense?
