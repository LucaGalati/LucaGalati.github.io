When a company’s loss costs its owner, do the books change?

Italian cities own companies: the ones that run the buses, collect the waste, pipe the water. For years a loss in one of them barely touched the city that owned it. A municipal budget counts cash, and a loss inside a company changed nothing in it, until the company could no longer pay its way and had to be rescued.

Then Italy changed the rule. From the 2015 accounts, when a company owned by a city reports a loss, the city must set aside part of its next budget, in proportion to its stake. Money set aside is money the mayor cannot spend.

The rule was meant to protect public services. It also tied the mayor’s budget to a number the company writes down itself. Did that number start to change?

What is a municipally owned entity? →

Article 21 of legislative decree 175 of 2016, applied from the 2015 financial statements; before it, the cash-based budgets of Italian municipalities ignored losses in the entities they owned until a recapitalisation was needed (paper, p. 344–345, 348–349). The services named are from the paper’s list (p. 348).

A loss now comes with a price tag

Under the rule the city sets aside, in its budget for the following year, its share of the company’s loss: a 60% owner of a company that loses €100,000 sets aside €60,000. The paper calls this a proportional appropriation.

Try it. Set the stake to 100% and the result to a loss of €10,000, then push the result just past zero and watch the city’s budget. Then do the same with a 10% stake.

The rule as the paper describes it: the amount depends on the size of the loss and on the share owned, and it enters the year after the loss (p. 345, 349, 353); this toy follows that description and leaves the decree’s finer points aside. Toy numbers: the company’s result, the €5 million budget and the worked example.

More stake, more reason, and above half, the power

Slide the city’s stake from 1% to 100% and watch two things: what a euro of loss that vanishes from the company’s accounts is worth to the city, and who names the company’s board.

From these two the paper draws its predictions about earnings management, the use of the room that accounting rules leave to steer a reported result. After the rule there should be more of it in these companies, and more of it the larger the city’s stake.

Why does owning more than half matter? →

Predictions 1 and 2: p. 347–348; earnings management: p. 346. The mayor names and removes directors, with very wide discretion when the city owns more than half: p. 347 and note 2; no formal say below 50%: p. 357. The paper’s groups: p. 349. The spending freed is the rule’s arithmetic, not an estimate.

Place a bet: did losses become rarer?

The paper follows the same 835 companies in which a city holds more than half, for five years before the rule and five years under it. From 2010 to 2014, 30.4% of them reported a loss.
