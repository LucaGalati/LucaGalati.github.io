A ruler that stretches

Let an amount grow by a steady percentage. To get from 1 to 2 it has to double; to get from 9 to 10 it needs only an 11% rise. So it spends far longer with a leading 1 than with a leading 9, and the same happens between 10 and 20, 100 and 200, and at every size above.

On a ruler where each step means multiplying rather than adding, the space each first digit owns is exactly its share under Benford’s law. Numbers that range over many sizes and are assembled from other numbers, like the net incomes of thousands of companies, spread evenly along that ruler, so their first digits come out in these shares.

A bad year moves the count, not the digits

Here are 2,320 invented companies. Each one’s result is its turnover times its margin, and the year sets the average margin. Slide from a bad year to a good one.

Watch two things as you go: the share of companies in the red, and the spread of their first digits.

The paper leans on a theorem for what you see: multiply a quantity that follows the law by an independent random factor, a business cycle for instance, and under mild conditions the product follows the law too. That is why it prefers digits to counts. A count of losses answers to the economy as readily as to the books; by this theorem, the spread of first digits does not.
