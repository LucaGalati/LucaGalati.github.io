If a firm writes more about its people, does it treat them better?

Every year, large companies in the European Union publish a report that is not about money: how they treat their employees, their customers, the towns they work in. Until 2017 writing one was a choice. Since then, for large listed firms, it has been the law.

Rating agencies, meanwhile, give each firm a social score meant to judge its practices, not its prose. This paper sets the two side by side for Italian listed firms: the words in the reports, and the ratings.

Do firms that write more about social matters score better on them? And once every firm has to write, does writing more still mean anything?

In 2017, writing about people became compulsory

The EU’s Non-Financial Reporting Directive took effect in 2017. Listed firms with at least 500 employees must now report on social matters such as gender equality, working conditions and community development, with wide latitude over what to say and how.

The study takes Italian firms in the FTSE Italia All-Share, the broad index of the Milan stock exchange, with at least 500 employees and a published report, five years either side of the rule: 329 reports from 83 firms.

The halves are not the same size: about 60 reports from before 2017, 269 from after. The paper reads this as fewer firms publishing a report before it was compulsory.

A computer counts the social words

Nobody in the study reads the reports for meaning. A script counts. It carries a published list of social words in four groups, public health, human rights, labour standards and society (Baier, Berninger and Kiesel, 2020). A report’s social tone is the number of listed words divided by the number of all its words.

Switch between a good year and a bad year, or type your own paragraph. The good year and the bad year score exactly the same: the count sees how much a report talks about people, not whether the news is good. The paper measures the mood of the prose separately, as net tone.

On the other side: a rating of what firms do

The other number is a social score from Refinitiv Eikon, a financial data service. It rates a firm’s labour practices, human rights, community engagement and customer satisfaction, and is meant to judge what the firm does rather than how its report reads.

In the sample, the middle half of reports score between 0.59 and 0.81. Set tone beside score and the two move together, but loosely: their correlation is 0.23.

Loose, and not yet fair: big firms and small, banks and utilities, 2013 and 2020 are all mixed up. The next deck compares like with like.
