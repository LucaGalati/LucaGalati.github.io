An explorable explanation

Can you spot touched-up accounts without opening them?

Italian towns own companies that run their buses, deliver their water, gas and electricity and collect their rubbish. Every spring those companies publish their accounts. In towns that vote that year, the election follows within weeks.

A healthy profit reads like proof of a well-run town. The mayor does not write the accounts, but can appoint or dismiss the company’s director, and the town is usually its biggest customer. So do accounts published just before a local election look different from the rest? Nobody outside can audit thousands of them. But honest numbers share a strange regularity that anyone can check.

Numbers spread over many powers of ten favour small first digits

Picture a ruler on which every power of ten has the same length: 1 to 10, 10 to 100, 100 to 1,000. To get from 1 to 2 a number must double; to get from 9 to 10 it grows by a ninth. So in every power of ten the stretch where numbers start with 1 is long, and the stretch for 9 is short.

Crowd the toy amounts together, then spread them out, and watch the share that starts with 1.

The paper’s profits run from 1 to 229,809,000, more than eight powers of ten, and their mean is about eleven times their median: just the kind of data that is very likely to follow the law when nobody touches it.

A detector you can operate

The paper measures how far a pile strays from the law with two yardsticks. The chi-square adds up the squared gaps, weighted by how rare each digit is; the adjusted MAD (mean absolute deviation, from Cerqueti and Lupi, Lupi being one of this paper’s authors) adds up the plain gaps, each scaled by how much that digit normally wobbles.

Each comes with a p value: how often an honest pile of the same size would stray at least this far by luck alone. Below 0.05, one time in twenty, the paper treats the pile as not following the law.
