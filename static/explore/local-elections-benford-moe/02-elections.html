Two piles of accounts

The authors merged a database of Italian company accounts with the Treasury’s open data on who owns what. They kept companies more than half owned by a town and working in five utility industries: public transport, gas, water, electricity and waste collection. Over a decade that leaves 5,021 positive pre-tax profits, about two election cycles per company.

Then they split the profits by the calendar. A statement is pre-election when it covers the year before the town’s election and comes out just before the vote. That gives 1,057 pre-election statements and 3,964 ordinary ones.

A smaller pile is not why it fails

An obvious objection: the pre-election pile is smaller, so maybe it is just noisier. But a smaller pile makes these tests less sensitive, not more. The ordinary pile, nearly four times bigger, had the better chance of being flagged, and it passed.

The authors also check directly. They draw 1,057 statements from the ordinary pile, the size of the pre-election one, run the test, and repeat 10,000 times. That shows how far an ordinary pile of that size strays by chance. Draw them yourself.

A misfit is a warning light, not a verdict

The tests say that pre-election profits stop looking like untouched numbers. They cannot say more, and the authors are careful about it.

  • Not necessarily illegalFor the paper, manipulation includes judgement calls inside the accounting rules, made to hit a goal rather than to give a fair view (p. 5).
  • No directionThe paper does not try to tell whether profits were pushed up or down, only whether they look touched (p. 4).
  • Whose motiveA manager may adjust the accounts for personal reasons, with no pressure from the mayor; the paper leaves that to future research (p. 6).
  • Head to headCompared directly, without the law, the two piles give p = 0.064, just above the 0.05 line: a rejection only at a level “just above the conventional 5%” (p. 4). Fully owned companies: p = 0.008.
  • What was testedFirst digits of positive pre-tax profits of Italian utility companies more than half owned by a town, over a decade (pp. 2, 4).

Deck 2 · Before the vote · 6 of 6

What to take away

Over a decade of accounts, the profits of Italian town-owned utilities fail two tests of Benford’s law just before local elections; in ordinary years they pass.

  1. Ordinary-year profits conform; pre-election profits do not (p = 0.016 and 0.033, companies more than half owned by a town).
  2. A smaller pile is not the reason: the bigger ordinary pile passes, and subsamples of the pre-election size rarely stray as far.
  3. Fully owned companies show a clearer misfit (p = 0.0003 and 0.0054).
  4. It signals lower data quality around elections, not proof of fraud: auditors should look harder then, and voters and media should not read these results as the mayor’s report card.

Compared directly, the two piles land just above the 0.05 line (p = 0.064); the fully owned piles differ clearly (p = 0.008). The tests cannot say which way profits moved, or who moved them.

Left to further research: was it the mayor’s pressure or the manager’s own motives (p. 6)? The authors’ advice: shield managers from politicians through more transparent appointments, as the OECD guidelines recommend (p. 6).
