When the window was one minute

Until 2015 the fix was taken from a single minute around 4 pm. In 2013, investigations at major banks showed how easily traders had moved it, colluding with each other and front-running their own clients’ orders. In 2014 the UK’s Financial Conduct Authority fined five banks £1.1 billion.

After a consultation started by the Financial Stability Board, the window was stretched in 2015 to five minutes centred on 4 pm, and Refinitiv set up a surveillance team at the regulator’s request.

Studies the report cites found that the change helped. Price reversals after the fix mostly disappeared, and banks spread their orders more evenly through the new window, though that opened new profit opportunities for non-bank traders. The cost: a five-minute fix is harder to replicate exactly.

A push into the fix, and the snap back

Suppose someone pushes the price up while the fix is being taken, then stops. Once the window closes, the price tends to slide back. Over many days that leaves a fingerprint: the move into the fix and the move after it are negatively correlated.

Evans (2018) reported evidence of that fingerprint in 21 currency pairs from 2000 to 2013; after the 2015 reform, Evans et al. (2018) found reversals had mostly gone.

The report’s test takes average prices in the 15 minutes before the fix (p1), during it (p2) and in the 15 minutes after (p3), and checks, quarter by quarter, whether the correlation between (p2 − p1)/p1 and (p3 − p2)/p2 is zero.
