Three rulers for one number

Before testing the worry, the report asks how good the fix is at all, and whether a longer window would do better. Three rulers from Evans, O’Neill, Rime and Saakvitne (2018) judge fixes computed over five, ten and twenty minutes.

  • RepresentativenessHow far is the fix from the day’s median trade price?
  • AttainabilityHow far is it from the mean price in the window, roughly what a trader spreading orders evenly through it would get? Can the fix be replicated?
  • RobustnessHow far is it from a “dirty” fix computed without the checks that drop outlying, possibly manipulative trades?

Each gap is squared, averaged over the days and square-rooted: one number per currency and window.

Thirty minutes either side, five seconds at a time

The rulers judge the number. The report also watches the market that produces it, and then asks whether the run-up predicts the fix. From half an hour before 4 pm to half an hour after, it cuts trading into five-second buckets and measures each one:

  • the bid–ask spread
  • the cost of a round trip
  • the 20-second price impact
  • the depth at the best prices
  • trade and quote counts
  • volatility

Then two comparisons, each with t-tests: ordinary days against month-ends, the last trading weekday of the month; and month-ends year by year, to see whether behaviour around the fix has changed.

A switch for month-ends

The direct test of the worry is a regression, a fit of the fix on the market measures. The fix of each pair on each day is set against those measures in seven five-minute slots, from half an hour before 4 pm up to the window itself, plus a switch, K, that is on only at month-end. If the month-end terms carry weight, behaviour ahead of the window says something different about the fix at month-end.

Controls: volatility in the window and the day’s macro-news intensity, 1 to 3; fixed effects absorb what is constant for a pair or a period.

What this design cannot tell you

The authors name these limits themselves, and say what even a null answer would be worth. Keep them in mind before reading any result this plan produces.

  • No experimentThe window has not changed since 2015, so there is no before and after to exploit, as Evans et al. (2018) could for the 2015 change. The plan compares kinds of days and years instead.
  • No trader identitiesWithout trader-level data, the earlier study cannot be fully replicated.
  • No volumes for every pairSo no volume-weighted prices. The authors note that WM/R ignores volume too, and argue that size says less about who is informed in currencies than in shares.
  • A null answer counts tooThe plan says in advance that finding no gain from a longer window would still be of interest to the industry.

What to take away

Five minutes of a market that never closes set the rate at which much of the world values its currencies. Do they still do the job? This report plans how to find out.

  1. The fix is the midpoint of a median bid and a median ask, from one trade picked each second in five minutes centred on 4 pm, a window widened from one minute in 2015, after the scandals.
  2. The worry: trading crowds into the window (in the authors’ data, about one GBP/USD dollar in eighteen, early 2023), above all at month-ends, when funds hedge at the fix. If prices move well before 4 pm, the fix may stop representing the market; a regression with a month-end switch checks this.
  3. The plan judges the fix with three rulers for windows of five, ten and twenty minutes, and watches the market around 4 pm on ordinary days and month-ends, 2015 to 2023.
  4. It says in advance what the answers would mean: longer windows doing better would point to a method that could be improved; no gain would not support a change, and would still be worth knowing.

A pre-registered report: no results here, and none implied. No natural experiment, no trader identities, no volumes for every pair; the findings are to be reported in a later article.

A question to take with you: funds, regulators and central banks all lean on the fix, and the report says they want different things from it. If a longer window turned out harder to push but harder to replicate, who would gain, and who would pay?
