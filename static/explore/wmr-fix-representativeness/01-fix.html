A market that never closes still needs a closing price

A stock exchange shuts every evening, and its last price becomes the price of the day. The currency market has no closing time: it trades around the clock, about $7.5 trillion a day in April 2022, against roughly $551 billion traded in equities over the same period.

So the industry made a close. Every afternoon at 4 pm London time, WM/Reuters publishes a rate for each currency pair, the WM/R fix. Funds value what they hold abroad at it, and index providers build it into multi-currency equity and bond indices. In 2014 the Financial Stability Board called it “by far the dominant benchmark”.

One number, taken from a market that never stops. How is it made, and is it still a fair picture of that market?

Place a bet before the money is drawn

The fix uses only trades inside a window of five minutes, from 3:57:30 to 4:02:30 pm London time. On a 24-hour clock, five minutes are about a third of one percent of the day.

Three hundred snapshots and a median

A trade is a buy at the ask, the lowest price on offer, or a sell at the bid, the highest price anyone will pay; the gap is the spread. Today the fix for the 39 currencies WM/R classes as traded takes four steps:

  1. Take the five minutes from 3:57:30 to 4:02:29.999 pm, London time.
  2. Every second, pick a trade at random and run it through validation checks: up to 300 snapshots.
  3. Turn each into a bid and an ask. A buy gives the ask; subtract the spread showing at that moment to get the bid. A sell, the other way round.
  4. Take the median bid and the median ask, separately. After a last check, their midpoint is the fix.

Section 1.2, page 2; 300 is computed. Less traded currencies use quotes instead, sampled every 15 seconds. The toy picks one of each second’s trades, always has one, and leaves the validation checks out. A pip is 0.0001.

The worry: the market moves before the window opens

Eight years after the reform, Refinitiv was preparing a new consultation, and warnings had piled up: from an ECB currency group uneasy about month-end trading at the fixes to a senior central banker who said funds might be letting their clients down, with currency swings twice as large as before.

The authors’ hypothesis: when unusual moves well ahead of 4 pm drive the trading around the fix, the fix stops representing the market. Their yardstick is the day’s median trade price, the middle of all the day’s trades. Look first at month-ends, when funds rebalance their currency hedges at the fix.
