490 sets of accounts published in an election year

The figures come from the AIDA database of Italian company accounts, as gathered for an earlier study of the same companies (Capalbo and coauthors, 2021): 506 utilities in public transport, gas, water, electricity and waste collection, in each of which a single town holds more than half the shares. With a majority, the paper argues, the town’s incumbents can sway management more easily, and the political stakes are likely to be higher.

Each company reports one revenue figure a year from 2009 to 2014: 3,036 in all. Accounts published in the year the town votes cover the year before; the paper calls them election-year accounts. There are 490 of them, from 422 companies, against 2,546 from other years.

The two groups are alike in size: a median of €3.6 million in election years and €3.0 million otherwise.

Place a bet: do the election-year digits still fit the law?

To judge all ninety bins at once, the paper uses the mean absolute deviation (MAD): for each bin, the gap between the share observed and the share Benford’s law expects, averaged over the ninety. Following Nigrini (2020), a MAD from 0.0012 to 0.0018 counts as acceptable conformity, up to 0.0022 as marginal, and anything above as nonconformity; lower values mean close conformity.

All 3,036 figures give 0.00157; the 2,546 from other years, 0.00158. Both acceptable. Where do the 490 election-year figures land?

Not a single 59, not a single 99

Just as telling, for the paper, is what is missing. Not one of the 490 election-year figures starts with 59, 85, 96 or 99. The paper reads the empty 59 and 99 bins as rounding up past a reference point, as Carslaw described: a figure a little under, say, €6 million or €100 million pushed over the line.

These are small bins: in 490 figures Benford’s law expects about 3.6 to start with 59 and 2.1 with 99. Table 5 gives the empty bins Z statistics of 1.633 and 1.123, short of the 10% bar.

The paper presents the missing 59s and 99s after its MAD result, as a further pattern in the same accounts, and notes that 85 and 96 are missing too.

From the ballot box to the digits

The paper’s conclusion: just before the elections, managers under pressure from political cost rounded revenue off to a cognitive reference point (pp. 128–129).

  1. the vote
    A town holds a local election.Eight election dates, 2010 to 2015 (p. 116).
  2. the motive
    Its incumbents want their companies to look well run.The political cost hypothesis, from the literature (pp. 106, 109).
  3. who
    Managers feel the pressure.The paper places it with the managers; the role of incumbents is left to future work (pp. 125, 128).
  4. the rounding
    Revenue is rounded up past a round number.No figure starts with 59 or 99; 88 and 42 are over-used, which the paper finds hard to explain (Table 5, pp. 124, 128).
  5. the digits
    The digits drift from Benford’s law.MAD 0.00354, past 0.0022 (Table 5). The K-S statistic, 0.0216, stays below 0.0614, in contrast with the MAD (p. 127).

The paper presents Benford’s law as a red flag rather than a fraud detector: a cheap first screen that the bodies guarding public money, such as Italy’s Corte dei Conti, could run on town-owned companies around elections.

It also names its own limits: the findings cannot be extended to state-owned enterprises in general; a plain feature of the accounts, such as standardised contracts, can make some digits repeat; the period studied may not be typical; and its tests point in different directions, an ambiguity it leaves to future work.

What to take away

Revenue digits drifted from Benford’s law past the paper’s yardstick in the 490 accounts published in an election year, but not in the other 2,546.

  1. Revenue figures, like many real-world numbers, start with small digits far more often than large ones; the paper’s 3,036 figures follow that pattern overall.
  2. Before local elections the fit breaks by the paper’s main yardstick (MAD 0.00354 against 0.0022). The paper reads this as consistent with the political cost hypothesis and with rounding up: 59 and 99 are left empty. The over-used 88 and 42 it finds hard to explain.
  3. The method flags rather than proves: the paper offers it to auditors such as the Corte dei Conti as a red flag, not a fraud detector.

Limits the paper names: its tests disagree (on Kolmogorov–Smirnov, which compares running totals, the widest gap, 0.0216, stays below the 0.0614 bar), an ambiguity left to future research; the results cannot be extended to state-owned enterprises in general; the period studied may not be typical.

Open, as the paper says: did incumbents actually lean on managers, and would a longer run of elections show the same drift?
