One number for ninety gaps

Two digits beat one for more than bluntness: people asked to invent numbers can produce first digits that fit the law surprisingly well (p. 118). So, following Nigrini (2020), the paper judges all ninety two-digit bins at once.

The mean absolute deviation (MAD) adds up, bin by bin, the gap between the share observed and the share expected, and divides by ninety. The paper’s tables print only some of the bins, for reasons of space, but the MAD fixes the total of all ninety gaps, so the part held by the other bins can be worked out.

Switch samples: in each, the printed bins carry well under half of the total; the rest is spread over the many bins not printed.

A Z statistic, step by step

A Z statistic asks how surprising one bin’s gap is, given how many figures there are. Its yardstick is the gap that chance alone would typically produce in a sample of that size: the rarer the bin and the smaller the sample, the larger that typical gap.

Pick any bin the paper prints for the election years. The steps are the paper’s formula filled in with that bin’s numbers.

The small correction, one over twice the sample size, allows for counting in whole figures; the paper drops it when it would be larger than the gap itself.

    A third test, a different answer

    A third test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, looks at the shape as a whole. Walk along the bins from 10 to 99 keeping a running total: the share of figures starting with 10, then with 10 or 11, and so on. Benford’s law gives one running total and the data another; the test statistic is the widest gap between them.

    For 490 figures the paper sets the 5% bar at 1.36 ÷ √490 = 0.0614. The widest gap in the election-year figures is 0.0216, about a third of that. On this test alone, the paper notes, there might be no sign of earnings management, a result in contrast with the MAD (p. 127).

    The paper leaves this disagreement among its tests to future research (pp. 120, 129). It also warns that such tests grow stricter as samples grow: the lower row shows how the bar falls.
