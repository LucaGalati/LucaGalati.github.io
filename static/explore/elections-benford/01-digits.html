Can the first two digits of a revenue figure tell you an election is coming?

Italian towns own companies that run their buses, supply water, gas and power, and collect the rubbish. In the year a town votes, those companies publish their accounts shortly before the ballot, and the people in charge may want them to look good.

This paper looks at those accounts in an unusual way: not at how big the numbers are, but at which digits they start with.

€5940,000 → €6000,000

Nudge a revenue figure from €5.94 million to €6.0 million and it grows by only one percent, but it now starts with 60 instead of 59. Do that often enough and the pattern of first digits shifts. Did it shift before local elections?

The euro figures are a toy example, not from the paper. Setting: the paper’s introduction and Section 3 (pp. 106–110, 116); the accounts are approved and published soon before the vote (p. 128).

Two digits, ninety bins

One digit is a blunt tool. Take the 490 sets of accounts published in a year the town voted (more on them shortly): even there, the share starting with 1 looks ordinary, 147 of 490, or 30.0%.

So the paper reads the first two digits, from 10 to 99. That makes ninety bins, each with its own share under Benford’s law, from 4.1% for 10 down to 0.44% for 99. The paper’s 3,036 figures sit close to that curve.

Slide along the bins. In a sample the size of the election-year one, the rarest bins should hold only two or three figures.

Rounding up leaves holes and heaps

In 1988 Carslaw found that New Zealand companies reported income with too few 9s and too many 0s as the second digit: figures just short of a round number had been pushed over it, to what he called a cognitive reference point. Studies of firms in the United States, Britain and Japan found the same habit.

The paper expects the same move when a vote is near. Try it on 490 toy revenue figures shaped to Benford’s law: push the ones just below a round number over the line.

Each nudge empties a bin ending in 9 and swells the round bin above it; 99 rolls over into 10, as €99 million becomes €100 million.
