I · 1 The question

Where is the price of a bond?

An EU-Bond — debt issued by the European Union — trades on many exchanges at once. Each is a lit venue: it shows its prices before anyone trades, in its own order book, the list of offers to buy and to sell. So one bond has many prices, one on every screen.

Everything that should move the price of a top-rated bond is public: a central-bank decision, a fiscal announcement. So the screens ought to agree, instantly. Do they? And if a regulator obliges dealers to keep quotes alive on designated screens, does any good come of it?

I · 2 The tension

Dispersed flow, stale screens.

More venues means more competition for the same orders, and competition tends to narrow spreads. But each venue sees only a slice of the flow. In this toy a screen moves only when an order reaches it, so split a day's orders across many screens and, after news, most of them sit on the old price for a while.

Try one screen, then seven, then fourteen. News arrives every morning at ten; press the button for more.

A toy with made-up numbers, not the paper's model: thirty orders a day, arriving at random and shared equally among the screens. It shows only the cost of more venues. Real screens update their quotes far more often than they trade, so the toy exaggerates how long they stay stale: it shows the direction, not the size.

I · 3 The intervention

Late 2023: the quoting arrangement.

In late 2023 the European Commission tried something other than removing screens. To make EU-Bonds easier to trade, it obliged their primary dealers to keep two-way quotes — a price to buy and a price to sell — alive on designated electronic platforms, during set hours, for minimum sizes and within maximum spreads.

The rule covered one issuer. Comparable top-rated bonds from KfW, the European Investment Bank, the World Bank and NWB Bank trade on the same exchanges and were left alone.

I · 4 The design

Why this counts as an experiment.

Studies of fragmentation usually struggle because venues and liquidity choose each other: an illiquid bond may scatter across venues, or the scatter may make it illiquid. Here the date was set by a regulator, the rule names one issuer, and both groups keep trading on the same exchanges, so whatever hits a venue hits both.

The paper pairs each treated EU-Bond with a control bond of the same currency and coupon type and a similar maturity, and compares how the two groups changed after the arrangement.
