III · 1 The friction

The price of a trade.

The price impact of a trade is how far the price moves in the trade's direction: a purchase pushes it up, a sale pushes it down. The thinner the order book, the bigger the push.

When liquidity moves from one venue to another, there is a stretch when it is split: the old book has thinned and the new one has not yet filled. Slide the liquidity across and send the same order each time.

The paper's price-impact results are mixed, and it reads them as a transition cost: the price of liquidity reallocating across venues — a cost that should fade once liquidity has settled.

III · 2 The limits

What the data cannot see.

  • Only lit venues. Trading can move between the exchanges in the data and the over-the-counter and dealer-to-client platforms outside it, unseen. A shift to the dark would look like a change in what the lit venues show.
  • A handful of bonds. The comparison rests on a few treated bonds and their controls. That limits statistical power, and with so few bonds the usual standard errors can come out too optimistic.
  • No private information. The classic measures of price discovery are reinterpreted rather than discarded, and the efficiency score is the paper's own construction.
  • A draft. The paper is in preparation, and its numbers can still change.

III · 3 Sandbox

Play with the arrangement.

All the knobs. Add screens, change how many orders a day they share, and add a designated screen that dealers must keep quoted — the toy's version of the arrangement. In the toy it comes on top of the others: one more screen, not one fewer.

The designated screen is right the moment news arrives; the others still wait for an order. In the data, synchronisation across venues grew stronger. The toy cannot tell whether that is the designated screens themselves or the others following them, and the abstract does not say: a question you can now ask.

Expected wait: with M screens sharing r orders a day, the last of the k screens that wait for orders catches up after (M ÷ r) × (1 + ½ + … + 1/k) trading days on average; k is M, or M − 1 with a designated screen. All numbers are toy values.

So, does it do any good?

III · 4 The answer

So, does it do any good?

Some. EU-Bonds' public-information efficiency rose by 4.3 percentage points relative to comparable bonds, mostly because prices on different venues moved together more closely. Not without frictions: the price-impact results are mixed, which the paper reads as the cost of liquidity moving between venues.

Questions you can now ask

  • Did any trading move off the lit screens, where the data cannot follow?
  • Do the other screens now follow the designated ones?
  • Did trading as a whole become more concentrated, or did the designated venues become extra screens?
  • Do the frictions fade once liquidity settles?

All of this rests on a handful of bonds and a paper still in preparation: Luca Galati and Loriana Pelizzon, working paper. The 4.3 points come from the paper's abstract; every other number on these pages is a toy value.
