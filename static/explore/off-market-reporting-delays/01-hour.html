Can hiding a trade for an hour make prices smarter?

A FTSE 100 futures contract is, in effect, a bet on where the index of the largest UK companies will stand on a future date; one contract is called a lot. Most lots trade in the exchange’s public order book, where everyone sees each price as it is struck. The largest trades often go another way: two parties agree size and price in private and report the deal afterwards. These are block trades.

On ICE Futures Europe the parties to a block could ask for its publication to be held back for up to an hour: the dark hour drawn above. On 19 February 2018 the exchange took that hour away from the smaller blocks. More transparency, you might think. The authors suspect the hour was what drew traders who know something to blocks at all. So did the market end up better informed, or worse?

A block trade has two moments

Take one block, agreed at 10:00. If one of its parties asks for the delay and the trade qualifies, the exchange holds the report back and publishes it at 11:00. Move the clock and watch who knows what.

A delayed block therefore has two moments: the handshake, when the price is agreed and the trade is done, and publication, when everyone else is told. The hour between them is the dark hour. The paper looks at prices around both moments.

The clock is illustrative. The rules are the paper’s: a block is published at once unless a party asks for deferral and the trade qualifies; deferral lasts up to an hour, and the paper treats a delayed block as done one hour before it was published (p. 2054, 2056–2057, Appendix A).

Deck 1 · The dark hour · 3 of 5

Two stories about the dark hour

Everyone agrees the hour delays the news. The two stories differ on who trades blocks because of it, and so on what should happen once it is gone.

Story one

The hour only delays the news

  • Someone who knows something trades a block.
  • The market learns of it an hour later than it could have.
  • Take the hour away: the same traders keep trading blocks, and the market learns at once.
  • Prediction: blocks of that size still move prices for good.

Story two · the paper’s hypothesis

The hour brings informed traders in

  • Someone who knows something uses a block because the hour gives them time to act on it before the market sees the trade.
  • So blocks carry news.
  • Take the hour away: they hide in costlier ways, or stop trading.
  • Prediction: blocks of that size stop moving prices for good.

An earlier study by Frino (2021) found an extra price move when delayed blocks were finally published, and read the hour as a cost to the market. That reading assumes story one. A change in the rules can tell the two apart.

On 19 February 2018, the line moved

Before that day, any FTSE 100 futures block of 500 lots or more could ask for the hour. From that day, only blocks above 3,328 lots could; smaller ones had to be published at once. The hour itself stayed an hour.

So blocks of 500 to 3,328 lots lost their delay overnight, on the same contract and the same exchange, while larger blocks kept theirs. The smaller ones are the paper’s experimental group, the larger ones its control group. Anything else that changed in the market should show up in both; the effect of losing the hour, only in the first.

News is the part of a price move that stays

Prices jump around any large trade, and much of the jump fades within minutes. What a block teaches the market is the part that stays. So the paper compares the price 30 minutes after a block’s publication with the price 30 minutes before: the block’s permanent effect. It is small, so it is counted in basis points (bp), hundredths of a percent.

Blocks are split into buy-like and sell-like; for a sell-like block, a lasting move is a fall.
