At the handshake, the jump looks the same

Start where earlier research looked: the moment a block is done. Gemmill (1996) did this for blocks on the London Stock Exchange under different publication rules, and found no simple effect of the rules.

Here too the jump at the block, step 0, is about the same size before the change as after it, and the next step takes much of it back. Each point is an excess return: the average move at that step beyond the price’s usual drift.

Look only here and the hour seems not to matter. But this data set also records when each block was published.

Place your bet: what happened at publication?

On 19 February 2018 blocks of 500 to 3,328 lots lost the dark hour. Before, the 30 minutes either side of publication came an hour after the handshake. From then on publication was the handshake, so the same window now held the trade itself.

Story one says these blocks still carry their news, now all of it inside the window; story two says the news stopped coming. Around publication, how big was their lasting move after the change, compared with before?

Large blocks kept their hour, and most of their news

Other things moved during the year too: the EU’s MiFID II rules, in force since January 2018, were bedding in, and the mix of traders may have shifted. Blocks above 3,328 lots lived through all of it and kept their hour. Had their lasting move collapsed as well, the rule change would not be the explanation.

It did not: theirs stayed roughly where it was, while that of the smaller blocks vanished (buy-like) or halved (sell-like). Take the larger blocks’ change away from the smaller blocks’ and what is left is the part the rule change can claim.

A wider window, from 90 minutes before publication to 30 after, also takes in the handshake; there the gap grows.

Deck 2 · What the hour was worth · 4 of 5

The hour slows the news, and may bring it in

What the hour costs

For an hour after a delayed block is done, the market trades without knowing of it. When the block is finally published the price can move again, as Frino (2021) found: news the market could have had sooner. Seen this way, a delay makes prices less efficient by exactly its own length.

What the hour may buy

When the hour went, the lasting move of blocks of 500 to 3,328 lots vanished for buy-like blocks and halved for sell-like ones, while larger blocks that kept the hour changed little. The authors’ reading: traders with private information were “squeezed out” of the upstairs market, the private market for blocks. Without the hour they turn to costlier ways of hiding, to blocks large enough to keep it, or they stay away. The paper does not observe which.

Behind this sits an old argument by Grossman and Stiglitz (1980): if prices gave away every piece of information at once, nobody would pay to find it out, and prices would end up knowing less. The paper finds its results consistent with that view. A little darkness may be part of the price of informative prices.

What to take away

Better informed, possibly, is the paper’s answer: the hour holds news back, but without it much of that news may never reach prices through blocks. Once blocks of 500 to 3,328 lots lost the hour, their lasting move around publication fell from about 5 bp to nothing measurable for buy-like blocks and halved for sell-like ones; blocks that kept it changed little.

  1. Around the handshake the jump was the same size under both rules; only at publication did the rule change show.
  2. Blocks that lost the hour carried much less lasting news. The paper reads this as informed traders leaving, an inference from prices, not from watching traders.
  3. A delay cuts both ways: it holds news back for an hour, and may bring in the informed trading that makes prices informative. That rests on one contract, one exchange, one year, and a control group of 64 to 79 much larger blocks per cell.

Open questions: where did the informed traders go? And when the threshold was lowered to 2,048 lots in September 2020, did the news come back to blocks of that size?
