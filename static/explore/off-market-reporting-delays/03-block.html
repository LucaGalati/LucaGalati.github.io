Upstairs, away from the screen

In the order book, the “downstairs” market, a buy order takes the cheapest offers first, then the next cheapest, and so on. That is fine for 20 lots. For 1,000 it climbs the book and pays a little more at every level it empties. Try it.

A block avoids the climb. Two parties agree one price for the whole size in private, upstairs, and one of them submits the deal to the exchange. On ICE a FTSE 100 futures block needed at least 500 lots, and a broker could not add up different clients’ orders to get there. The price need not match the screen; it only has to be fair for a trade of that size.

Unless deferral is requested and allowed, the exchange publishes the block at once. Blocks may only be done in trading hours, 1 a.m. to 9 p.m. London time, never overnight.

Buy or sell, read from the price

For trades on screen the data say which side started the trade. For blocks they do not; the exchange keeps that to itself. So the authors infer it from the price. They compare each block’s price with that of an earlier on-screen trade; for a delayed block, the trade at the handshake, an hour before the block was reported. Above it, the block counts as premium, buy-like; below it, discount, sell-like; at the same price it is a zero-tick and is dropped. Nine blocks were.

Why keep them apart? Trades started by buyers tend to carry good news and trades started by sellers bad news, so the two kinds are studied separately, each with its own sign.
