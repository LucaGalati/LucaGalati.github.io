An explorable explanation · De Blasis, Galati & Petroni

If you knew which stocks lead, would you choose a better portfolio?

Since Harry Markowitz in 1952, the classic recipe for a portfolio has needed two ingredients: what each stock tends to earn, and how stocks move together. “Together” is measured by correlation, and correlation is symmetric. It can say that two stocks rise and fall as a pair; it cannot say which of them moves first.

Yet some stocks lead. When one stock’s move today says something about another’s tomorrow, information travels in a direction. Draw those directions between all the stocks of an index and you have a map of who leads whom.

Can such a map tell you what to own? This paper builds one and tests it on twenty years of three stock markets.

Tomorrow, as a mix of today

The model, a mixture transition distribution (MTD), first sorts every trading day of every stock into one of three states: down, flat or up. Flat is any move within half a standard deviation of zero, too small to count as a signal.

Then it makes one assumption. A stock’s chances of being down, flat or up tomorrow are a weighted mix of what each stock’s state today suggests, its own included. The weights, written λ, are shares of influence: none is negative, and for each stock they add up to one.

The weights are fitted, not chosen: the best weight is the one that makes the history the model saw most likely. Find it for two invented stocks.

Deck 1 · Who leads whom · 3 of 4

Everyone is led about equally. Some lead far more.

Fit the weights for every stock of an index and you have a table of how much each stock pulls on each other. Leave out each stock’s pull on itself and the table becomes a network of arrows, thicker where the pull is stronger.

Two numbers describe each stock. Its in-strength adds up the arrows coming in: how much it is led. Its out-strength adds up the arrows going out: how much it leads, which the paper reads, once normalized, as its share of price leadership.

Switch the circles between the two. In-strength hardly varies, because the weights into each stock add up to one, less what it takes from its own past. Out-strength runs from 0.37 for H to 1.85 for E. Click a stock to see whom it leads.

Like with like, asked four ways

Popular children tend to befriend popular children. Network scientists call that assortative mixing; when opposites pair up instead, the network is disassortative.

With arrows the question comes in four versions, because every arrow has a source and a target, and each can be judged by how much it is led (in) or how much it leads (out). In–Out, for instance, asks whether arrows run from much-led stocks to strong leaders.

Each dot is one of the 28 arrows. Across: its source’s strength; up: its target’s, each counted without the arrow itself. The tilt of the cloud, a correlation weighted by the arrows, is the network’s assortativity.
