Each arrow is judged without itself

Stock E leads four stocks, and its out-strength, 1.853, adds up all four arrows. To ask whether the arrow from E to A joins a leader to a follower, the paper first takes that arrow out: on this arrow E’s excess out-strength is 1.853 − 0.669 = 1.184.

What is left, the paper says, is the influence outside this arrow. The target is judged the same way: its in-strength without the arrow that reaches it. Excess strengths are a common choice in work on assortativity since Newman (2003).

A stock’s score adds up its own arrows

A stock’s score, its local assortativity, is built from the arrows it sends. Each arrow counts its weight, times the source’s excess strength, times how far the target’s excess strength sits from the average target’s. The four are summed and divided by one scale shared by all ten stocks, so the ten scores add up to the network’s assortativity.

Under In–In, E’s arrows to A and J point at stocks led far less than the average target once E’s own arrow is set aside. They outweigh the rest: E scores −0.555, the lowest in Table 2. Switch the modality and the same arrows add up differently.
