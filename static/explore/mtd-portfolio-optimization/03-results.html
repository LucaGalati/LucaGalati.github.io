Seventeen of eighteen stay among the best

Are these gaps more than luck? The paper checks its Sharpe ratios with a Model Confidence Set (Hansen, Lunde and Nason, 2011): the group of portfolios that cannot be told apart from the best one at the 95% level.

Seventeen of the eighteen are in, the two baselines among them; only Out–Out on the FTSE 100 drops out. The authors read this as a sign that their findings are robust: almost every network rule, in every market, stays in the best-performing set.

What to take away

In these tests, more often than not. Over twenty years and three markets, tilting the classic recipe by where each stock sits in a network of who leads whom gave a higher average Sharpe ratio in seven of twelve tries, and in all three markets when In–Out links were avoided.

  1. The MTD model turns days of ups and downs into a directed, weighted network of who leads whom, which a correlation network cannot show.
  2. Minimizing In–Out, which favours stocks with clearly different roles, gave the most consistent gains over both yardsticks; Out–Out never helped. The authors read this as better diversification.
  3. The portfolios are small, three to seven stocks on average, with almost the same annual volatility under every rule.

The Model Confidence Set keeps seventeen of the eighteen portfolios, both baselines included, among the best at the 95% level. With the appendix’s second scoring method, In–Out wins in two markets of three. And in the authors’ words, results become “slightly less consistent” as markets grow and the network gets more complex.

A question to take with you: in the ten-stock network, which pair other than D and G ranks in the top ten of 45 under all four rules at once? Two do.
