A strong link is a short road

Before the real network, the paper’s own small example: five coins, a to e, and six links with their strengths.

To say how far apart two coins are, the authors turn each strength into a road length: one divided by the strength. A strong link of 0.5 is a road of 2; a weak one of 0.1, a road of 10. The distance between two coins is the cheapest way from one to the other, and it may well run through a third coin.

Two weak links or one strong one?

There is more than one way to be central:

  • Degreehow many links a coin has.
  • Closenesshow short its roads to all the others are.
  • Information centralityhow much the whole network slows when the coin’s links are cut.

Coin c has two links, 0.2 to a and 0.1 to b. Coin e has one, 0.4 to the hub a. Which matters more to the network?

Take a coin out and the whole network slows

Information centrality works by subtraction. First measure the network’s efficiency: one over the cheapest road for every pair of coins, averaged. Then cut one coin’s links and measure again. The share of efficiency lost is that coin’s information centrality.

Cut each coin in turn and fill in the bars. Cutting the hub a leaves only three roads standing, and they are long ones.
