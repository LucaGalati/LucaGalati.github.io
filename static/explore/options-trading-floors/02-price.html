Only about a third of floor trades have an electronic twin.

Size and legs decide where a package goes: a routing model with just those two fits nearly as well (pseudo-R² 0.70) as one with every other trait (0.80). So the paper pairs each floor trade from June 15–26 with its nearest electronic trade from June 1–14, within a quarter of a standard deviation on six traits: size, legs, days to expiration, implied volatility, delta and vega.

About a third of floor trades find a twin, and those pairs are where the 1.14-cent gap comes from: with controls, the floor charged them 36% more. The rest are bigger and far more complex. In the closure's last two weeks, no electronic trade came close enough on all six traits to be their twin.

Effective spread: the distance between the price paid and the midpoint of the leg quotes, in cents per unit of the index. Hedging the package's delta in futures costs 0.28 cents for these pairs, so nearly all of the spread is market-maker revenue.

Does the answer depend on the size of the trade?

Split them by size and the sign flips.

Averages mix very different trades. So the paper also compares every floor trade from June 15–26 with every electronic order from June 1–12, without matching, inside five bands of the floor size distribution, with the same controls as before.

For small packages the floor is clearly dearer: 3.49 cents more than electronic trades of the same size. In the middle, no reliable difference. For the largest tenth of floor trades, 1,949 contracts and up, the floor is cheaper by 4.35 cents. Market-maker revenue tells the same story.

The matched gap of 1.14 cents, the 36%, came from the lower half of the distribution, where the floor loses. The trades the floor exists for sit in the right tail. Harris (2003) predicted this division of labor: small option trades automate, and floors keep the large traders.

What if you must trade 1,950 contracts without a floor? →

The biggest floor trades have no twin, so the paper builds one.

Take a floor trade of 1,950 contracts, bigger than nine in ten floor trades. To do it on the screens, chain the electronic orders of June 1–12, one after another within a day, until they add up to 1,950 contracts. On average that takes 65 orders and 1.3 minutes.

Each piece pays the screen's price, about 3.12 cents per unit, so the bill grows in a straight line: $6,081 in spreads, plus $85–87 of futures slippage as the hedging of the growing delta moves the price. Floor trades of similar size paid between $2,439 and $4,246.

But is it the same trade? →

And the twin is a counterfeit.

The chain matches the size of a big floor trade, not its package. Floor trades without a twin average 17 legs, and 65 unrelated small packages do not add up to those legs. A trader who built the package leg by leg would be exposed on every leg still missing: the risk a package exists to remove.

A caution the paper raises itself: every cost here is measured on trades that executed, and a trade someone wanted but never made is invisible on both venues. For big trades that likely flatters the screen, since the few that executed electronically were probably the cheapest. Traders who could not wait for the floor may have paid more.
