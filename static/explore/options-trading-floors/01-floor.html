An explorable explanation of a research paper

Stock markets stopped shouting a long time ago.

On the New York Stock Exchange people still stand on the floor, but floor trading is roughly 3% of share volume, most of it in the opening and closing auctions. In stocks, the move to screens is essentially complete.

So guess: what share of U.S. options trading, by dollar value, still happens by open outcry, voice and hand signal on a trading floor?

More than a fifth. About three quarters of that is one product on one floor: options on the S&P 500 index, in Chicago.

On March 16, 2020, that floor closed for three months while the screens kept trading. Why would anyone still shout? And what did the market lose when the shouting stopped?

What trades there? →

Almost nothing on the floor is a simple “buy 100 calls”.

A complex order is one instruction to trade two or more options, called legs, at a single net price. Buy a call at $3.00 and sell a put at $1.00 as one package, and you pay $2.00 net.

The legs fill together or not at all. For legs meant to offset each other, the package hedges only if every leg executes. Try sending the same two legs separately.

Where do the big packages go? →

The floor is where the big packages go.

Right after it reopened, the average floor trade had 784 contracts and 12 legs. In the closure's last two weeks, the average electronic trade had 36 contracts and under 3 legs.

So the floor carries about 70% of the dollar volume in complex SPX trades but only 1.6% of the orders: a small number of very large packages.

How do you test what a floor is worth? →

A clean switch is rare in markets.

Usually traders choose their venue, and any comparison of venues carries the fingerprints of that choice. In 2020 the choice was taken away. On March 16 Cboe shut the floor and rejected every order marked for it. On June 15 the floor reopened.

The paper studies the reopening. In March the VIX, the market's volatility gauge, rose above 80; around June 15 it averaged about 29 in the two weeks before and 33 in the two weeks after, with no jump on the day. SPY packages, which track the same index on other exchanges and never touch the Cboe floor, showed no change in cost (−0.05 cents, statistically zero).

While the floor was shut, two temporary rules kept SPX packages trading on the screens:

  1. AIM, an electronic auction in which a broker brings an order already paired with a counterparty and others may improve the price, was switched on for SPX packages for the first time.
  2. Packages whose legs differ in size by up to 25 to 1 could trade electronically. The limit had been 3 to 1; anything beyond it had to go to the floor.

Both were reversed on June 15. The reopening brought back the floor and switched off AIM on the same day.

So what happened when the floor came back? →

When the floor came back, did comparable trades cost more there, or less?

The paper pairs each floor trade from June 15–26 with a look-alike electronic trade from June 1–14.

Cost is the effective spread: how far the price paid sits from the midpoint of the quotes, in cents per unit of the index. One cent on one contract is one dollar.

Matched floor trades paid 4.45 cents against 3.15 cents for their electronic twins. With market conditions and the traits of each trade held fixed, the gap is 1.14 cents, 36% of the electronic spread, and market makers kept it: their revenue was 1.15 cents higher. Against AIM trades alone the gap is about twice as large, 2.44 cents.

So the floor is an expensive habit? That is one reading. It is incomplete, and each of the two paths shows why.
