Path 3 · Inside the crowd

An auction and a crowd differ in three ways that matter for a big package.

On the screens, comparable trades were cheaper. Yet the biggest packages keep going to the crowd. The paper points to three differences: who must commit before anything happens, how the order can be divided, and what the parties know about each other. The first is written in the rule book; for the other two the paper brings evidence. Take them in any order.

seen 1 · Pre-commitment

Who must commit first

AIM, the electronic auction that drew the largest packages, cannot start until one firm guarantees the whole trade. The crowd needs no one lined up.

 seen 2 · Flexibility

How the order is divided

An algorithm allocates in fixed shares. The crowd sizes its own pieces, and shares the risk of the largest trades.

 seen 3 · Screening

What they know about each other

Faces are known on the floor. A broker can vouch for a client's motive, and the futures market hints at whether it works.

AIM cannot start until one firm guarantees the whole trade.

Why the screen struggles with a big package: market makers have no duty to quote packages, and in the book they mostly take liquidity rather than offer it. They start 94.5% of the trades there, by picking off orders that rest in it.

So without the floor, a large package trades in one piece only if a single firm commits to all of it, at a stop price, before the auction starts. On the floor nobody has to: the broker shows the order, and the crowd divides it.

The crowd sizes its own pieces.

A fill is one execution in one leg of a package. In AIM an algorithm allocates: the firm that started the auction gets up to 50% or 40% of the order, and many of its fills land exactly on those shares. On the floor several market makers can share an order at one price, and the pieces come in many sizes.

Drag the dial left toward small orders. There the floor more often hands a whole leg to one counterparty: 46.8% of fills against 29.9% in AIM. For the largest orders it almost never does: 1.3% against 3.9%. Each market maker takes the slice that fits its own book, holds less unhedged risk until it can hedge, and can quote a better price.

That is consistent with what the size dial shows: from 1,949 contracts up, the floor is 4.35 cents per unit cheaper.

Faces are known in the crowd.

A floor broker can tell the crowd why a client is trading and how much is left, and a client can build a reputation through the broker. A trader who can credibly show a liquidity motive separates from informed traders (Seppi, 1990), and repeated dealing lets a specialist discipline brokers who bring informed orders (Benveniste, Marcus and Wilhelm, 1992). If the floor screens, its trades should move the S&P 500 futures less than electronic trades with the same delta.

They do. Each $10,000 of signed delta moves the E-mini by 0.43 basis points within ten seconds when the trade is electronic; on the floor the same delta moves it 0.54 basis points less, so floor trades show no positive impact. Among the largest trades the gap is about one basis point.

The paper is careful here: the large-trade estimate rests on 672 trades; the gap is not significantly larger than for mid-sized trades; uninformed traders may simply choose the floor for their largest trades; and hedging pressure and the timing of the window can differ by venue. Suggestive, not conclusive.

So what is the floor for? → ← The three differences

When the floor closed, the trades it exists for became half as common.

About 24% of complex SPX dollar volume before the closure, about 13% during it, about 21% after. The weekly low, about 8.5%, came in the week of June 1: Cboe had announced on May 26 that the floor would reopen on June 8, then on June 3 pushed the date to June 15. The low is consistent with traders holding large trades back until the floor returned; weaker demand during the pandemic may explain part of the dip.

What did the market lose when the floor closed? About half of its largest, most complex trades. What did it gain? Cheaper execution for the smaller packages that screens handle well.

The floor facilitates the most difficult trades, which the exchange's electronic mechanisms execute at higher cost or not at all. It also raises the cost of the less difficult ones.

One example order from each size band. Send each where you expect it to cost less; the paper's estimate for its band appears as you choose.

Screens for the smallest, the floor for the largest, and in between the paper cannot tell the two apart. That is the division of labor Harris (2003) predicted.

Keeping the floor open while leaving AIM switched on for SPX packages could lower total costs, if orders go where they fit. Whether the floor would survive if its small trades left, and why no electronic auction offers the crowd's flexibility, the paper leaves open.

The trading floor is not dead yet.
