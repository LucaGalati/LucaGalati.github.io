Burn one, mint the other

UST had no vault of dollars. Its protocol would always swap 1 UST for $1 of newly created LUNA, burning (destroying) the UST, and $1 of LUNA for one new UST.

That handed a trade to anyone who spotted a gap. Below a dollar: buy UST cheap, swap it for $1 of LUNA, sell the LUNA. Each swap burns a UST, and fewer coins should lift the price. Above a dollar, the reverse.

The catch is the word “sell”. In a run, every UST handed in mints LUNA that someone then sells, so the run itself drags LUNA down: that is your second slider, and the extra loss the toy on deck 1 builds in. Once LUNA falls faster than UST’s discount, the swap loses money and the loop turns against the peg.

What kept people holding UST

A peg that rests on a swap needs people who want to hold the coin. UST’s draw was yield: lenders of UST were offered nearly 20% a year, when deposits of USDC earned 3.5–5.5% and dollar deposits at major American banks less than 1%.

The interest came out of UST’s reserves. The paper judges that the high rate was probably needed to keep demand for UST up, and that the peg depended on that demand.

The peg had slipped before, and come back. Most of the time UST traded just over a dollar, which the paper reads as a sign of some faith in the project.
