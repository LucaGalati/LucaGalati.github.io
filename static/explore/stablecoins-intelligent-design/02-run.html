A dollar for 95 cents, and few takers

If Tether sells for 95 cents and Tether itself takes coins back for a dollar, why not buy every coin on offer and hand them in? That redemption trade should drag the price back to $1.

Try it on the terms the paper reports: no less than $100,000 at a time, $1,000 per withdrawal at these sizes, 150 USDT to verify an account, and for most US persons no dollars at all.

The paper reads the fact that the discount was not closed at once as a sign that many traders feared a wider collapse.

The shock reached every coin

The paper’s formal test asks whether the way UST moved with each other asset changed after 10 May.

The measure is the covariance: positive when two coins’ returns tend to move the same way in the same minute, negative when they move opposite ways, near zero when they ignore each other. The test counts a shift either way, up or down, as a sign that the shock spread.

All six shifts clear the 1% bar: each t-statistic, the shift set against its noise, is beyond about 2.6. The paper reads this as contagion from UST’s collapse to every asset it studies, likely due in part to herding.

What to take away

Five coins made the same promise. When UST failed, traders treated them very differently, and the paper links the difference to how each was built.

  1. UST’s collapse spread: its co-movement with bitcoin and with every stablecoin studied shifted, significant at 1%. The paper reads this as contagion, likely due in part to herding.
  2. Design shaped the direction, the paper finds. Traders paid more than a dollar for the coins backed by cash and US government debt, sold Tether for less, and left DAI near a dollar: a flight to safety.
  3. It shaped the duration too. BUSD’s move faded; Tether stayed off its peg until 19 July; UST never recovered.

One event and forty days of minute prices averaged across exchanges, with herding offered as a likely part of the cause. The authors say their findings cannot be extended to calmer markets.

Where do the findings stop? →

Open questions the paper raises: could the pools of decentralised exchanges have warned of the crash two days early? Why do traders keep using the largest coins in calm times, when a crash shows they can tell which are safer?

To take with you: in the toy, crypto collateral breaks beyond a 31% fall, yet DAI held near $1 in a week when bitcoin’s CAR touched −50%. A CAR is not a price fall, but what might the toy leave out? And which of its designs is Tether closest to?
