The exchange’s own flag says which trades were blocks

Studies of stock markets usually have to guess what a block is: any trade above 10,000 shares, say, or the largest 1%. Here there is no guessing. The trades come from Gemini’s data interface, retrieved with CDD Media & Analytics, and each carries a flag saying whether it was a block.

  • Databitcoin against the dollar on Gemini, 6 December 2018 to 5 December 2020
  • Each tradeprice, size, dollar amount, the side that started it, time to a hundredth of a second, block flag
  • Kepttrades of 5 BTC or more, on the book or upstairs; auction trades dropped

The change shows in the block sizes: before it, most blocks were exactly 10 BTC; after it, 5 BTC blocks were the most common, with 10 close behind.

Which way did a block go, and when did it really happen?

Which way. A book trade is labelled with the side that started it. A block is not, so the paper uses the tick rule: a block priced above the book trade at the same moment counts as a buy, one priced below it as a sell.

When. The data stamp a block when it is printed. To look at the moment it was executed, the authors move that stamp back by the delay. Around each block they then take the twenty trades before execution and the twenty after the print: on average more than half an hour of trading. Twenty follows the classic studies of block trades; windows of 5 to 35 trades give similar answers.

Unseen, yet moving. Measured from its execution, a block’s lasting footprint comes close to a book trade’s, 0.116% against 0.139% for buys, although only the auction knows of it. The paper reads this as the mark the block would have left had it been printed at once.
