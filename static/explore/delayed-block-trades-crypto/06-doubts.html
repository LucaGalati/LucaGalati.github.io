How the paper keeps the comparison fair

The experiment compares 5 to 10 BTC trades with blocks over 10 BTC, before and after 6 December 2019. Here is what the paper does to keep that comparison fair, and where its evidence stops.

What the paper accounts for

  • A control group whose rules never changed, plus a second one of 5 to 10 BTC book trades; without the second, the results are almost identical.
  • Swings that hit everyone: a before-and-after marker, trading volume, volatility, a marker for COVID-19 and the 2020 bubble, bitcoin’s own return. The estimate barely moves.
  • Windows from 5 to 35 trades around each block: similar results.

Where the evidence stops

  • Around execution and print together, the decline is significant for sells but not for buys; the paper finds less evidence for buys there.
  • Nobody’s identity is in the data, so why the shift happened is the authors’ reading.
  • One exchange, one coin, one rule change.

A second method agrees: book buying moves the price, block buying barely

Instead of following single trades, the authors cut the two years into one-minute slices and use Hasbrouck’s (1991) vector autoregression to ask how bitcoin’s price responds, over the next ten minutes, to an extra $1 million of net buying through book trades, or through blocks.

Ten minutes on, book buying has lifted the price by about 0.2%; block buying by about 0.02%, with a band that includes zero. Split by period, the response to block buying fades to nothing before the change and leaves a small mark after it, while the response to book buying is the larger one before.
