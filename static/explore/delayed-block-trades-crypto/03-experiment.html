Place a bet: did the delay draw in traders who know something?

In futures markets, a delay gives a trader with private information time to use it before anyone sees the trade, so delayed blocks tend to be the informed ones (Frino, Galati and Gerace, 2022). The authors expected the same here: once 5 to 10 BTC trades could be printed ten minutes late, their permanent footprint should grow.

After the change, the average block moved the price more

A block facility exists to let large trades through without jolting the price. The total price impact is the whole move from twenty trades before to the block’s own price. After the change it grew: from 0.121% to 0.158% for block buys, from 0.085% to 0.123% for block sells.

For book trades, the total fell for buys and held steady for sells. The paper concludes that the lower threshold raised the blocks’ total market impact, the opposite of what the facility was introduced for.

Table 3 tests blocks before against after: t = 1.642, p = 0.101 for buys; t = −1.841, p = 0.066 for sells.

Deck 3 · The experiment · 4 of 5

Why might a delay keep informed traders away?

The authors read the drop as the delay discouraging informed trading. In futures markets a delay did the opposite: why would Gemini differ?

Upstairs in stocks and futures

Two parties strike a deal, often through a broker who screens clients. Where reporting can be delayed, the informed traders who do trade upstairs get time to use what they know (Frino et al., 2022).

Upstairs on Gemini

An order goes to every authorised market maker at once. The makers see a midpoint and a minimum, nothing else, and answer blind, as you did. Anyone with an account can send one.

The authors offer three readings:

  1. Blocks here work like a dark auction for market makers rather than a private deal.
  2. Traders whose information lasts may not need speed, and may prefer to work patiently through limit orders on the book (Collin-Dufresne and Fos, 2015).
  3. The lower threshold opened blocks to smaller, perhaps less sophisticated traders. With no data on who trades, the authors call this speculative.

Why have an upstairs market at all, if its trades carry less information? Madhavan and Cheng (1997) argued that in stock markets it mainly serves those who supply liquidity, not those who start the trade; the paper suggests the same may hold here.

Deck 3 · The experiment · 5 of 5

What to take away

On Gemini, a ten-minute delay did not draw informed traders upstairs; the authors read the evidence as the delay discouraging them.

  1. Large trades on the order book move bitcoin’s price for good more than blocks do once these are printed, as theory for upstairs markets predicts; the paper “largely” confirms it.
  2. Once 5 to 10 BTC trades could be delayed, their lasting price impact fell relative to the control group, the reverse of the futures evidence: delay discouraged informed trading, the authors conclude, potentially making the market less informative.
  3. The average block’s total price impact rose after the change, which the authors read as running against the purpose of a block facility (Table 3: p = 0.101 for buys, 0.066 for sells).

Around execution and print together the drop is significant for sells but not for buys, where the paper finds less evidence. The data do not say who traded, so the reasons are the authors’ reading. The evidence comes from one coin, one exchange and one rule change. The authors leave open whether the information in a trade rises or falls as trades get smaller, and whether crypto markets need theory of their own.
