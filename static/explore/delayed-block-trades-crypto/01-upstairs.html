If you could hide a big trade for ten minutes, who would want to?

Say you want to buy a lot of bitcoin in one go. On an exchange’s open order book, the public list of offers to buy and sell, your trade is printed for everyone to see the moment it happens. A big one can push the price against you, and it tells everyone else that somebody wanted a lot of bitcoin, fast.

On 6 December 2019 the Gemini exchange changed one number. Trades of 5 to 10 bitcoin, which until then went through the open book and were printed at once, could from that day be arranged away from the book, as a block, and printed ten minutes later.

Ten minutes of silence are worth most to someone who knows something the market does not know yet. In futures markets, a delay of this kind drew such traders in. Did it here?

Deck 1 · Going upstairs · 2 of 4

Downstairs, everyone sees at once. Upstairs, ten minutes later.

The open order book is downstairs: buyers and sellers post prices, trades happen against them and are printed straight away. A block facility is upstairs: large trades are arranged away from the book.

Gemini opened its block facility on 12 April 2018. A customer sends an indication of interest: side, size, the smallest fill they will accept, and a limit price within 5% of the book’s midpoint, halfway between the best offer to buy and the best offer to sell. It goes at once to every participating market maker, a firm that stands ready to buy or sell; an auction runs for a minute, and the result is printed for everyone ten minutes after it is executed. Any customer may send one; only market makers the exchange authorises may fill it.

You are the market maker. Nobody tells you which way the customer is going.

This is the paper’s own worked example. A customer has sent an indication of interest. As a market maker you see only the smallest fill, 10 BTC, and the book’s midpoint when it arrived: not the side, not the full size, not who is asking. On Gemini, the paper argues, a block is closer to an auction in the dark than to a private deal between two parties.

Three makers have answered. Add yours, buying or selling 10 BTC at a price of your choice, and send it. One minute later the exchange matches the answers. Then find out what winning was worth.

Deck 1 · Going upstairs · 4 of 4

One number changed, and only the middle trades changed with it

Until 6 December 2019 a bitcoin block had to be at least 10 BTC; from that day 5 BTC was enough. Slide a trade’s size and see where it could go in each year.

That is the paper’s experiment. Trades of 5 to 10 BTC are the treated group: printed at once before the change, free to go upstairs and be printed late after it. Blocks over 10 BTC are the control: delayed in both years, so they soak up whatever else changed in bitcoin between the two years. Trades under 5 BTC are left out.

The data run a year either side of the change, from 6 December 2018 to 5 December 2020: 18,084 trades of at least 5 BTC, 8.07% of them blocks.
