Every coin got jumpier

Contagion, in this paper, is about how coins move from one minute to the next. Start with how much they moved.

The size of a typical one-minute move is its standard deviation. In the week after Circle’s message it grew for all six assets: almost twelvefold for USDC, whose week after includes a few extreme minutes, and about threefold for Bitcoin. The paper reads the spikes as roughly simultaneous across coins and calls it volatility spilling over.

Getting jumpy in the same week is a first sign. The paper’s test asks something sharper: did the way the coins move together change?

Minute by minute, they barely move together. Except two.

Correlation measures how far two coins move the same way in the same minute: +1 always together, 0 unrelated, −1 always opposite. The paper gets it from the BEKK model, a standard tool that re-estimates every pair’s co-movement each minute; the grid shows each week’s average.

Almost every pair sits near zero. One reading: a coin pegged to a dollar wiggles around it, and different coins’ wiggles are mostly unrelated. One pair is the exception: Tether and the Binance dollar, at 0.96 in the week before and 0.99 after.

Place a bet: did the shock spread?

The paper’s test pairs USDC, the coin with the stuck reserves, with each of the other five assets, and compares the pair’s average covariance, co-movement that also grows with the size of the moves, in the week before and the week after.

Of the five pairs, how many changed by more than chance would explain, at the strict 1% level?

A t-statistic is the change divided by how uncertain the two averages are. Beyond about 2.6 in size, chance alone would produce one less than 1% of the time.

What to take away

A bank failed, and the trouble did not stay in banking.

  1. A “flight to safety”, in the paper’s reading: USDC and DAI sank, Tether rose.
  2. Panic, in the paper’s reading: trading surged in the coins in doubt, fell in BUSD.
  3. Contagion, in the paper’s reading: all five USDC links changed, each significant at the 1% level.

One event, two weeks, the six most liquid assets, prices averaged across exchanges, and a week before that already held two bank failures. A before-and-after comparison also takes in everything else that happened that week, including the rescue of depositors on 12 March.

Still open, as the paper notes: how far do these links between centralised and decentralised finance reach, beyond the most liquid coins?
