When a bank fails, does the trouble stay in banking?

A stablecoin is a crypto token built to be boring: one coin, worth one US dollar, always. At about 03:11 UTC on Saturday 11 March 2023, Circle, the company behind USD Coin (USDC), the second-largest stablecoin, said that 8% of the coin’s reserves were stuck at Silicon Valley Bank, which regulators had taken over the day before. Within hours one USDC traded below 87 cents.

SVB was a commercial bank, banker to nearly half of Silicon Valley’s venture-backed tech start-ups. Did its failure spread into crypto markets? And when USDC holders took fright, where did their money go?

The arithmetic of a stuck reserve

Each USDC is meant to be backed by a dollar in reserve. About $40 billion stood behind the coin, and $3.3 billion of it sat at SVB, where withdrawals had been halted. Nobody knew that weekend how much would come back.

News reports had USDC below 87 cents. If a coin were worth just the reserves behind it, how much of the stuck money would have to be lost to justify that price? Try it.

The paper’s own prices, a weighted average across exchanges that the authors note smooths the moves, fell less far: in USDC’s worst three hours they sat around 91.6 cents, dipping to about 88. And a price carries more than arithmetic: holders feared they could not get their money out, and the paper reads prices this far from a dollar as worry about the stability, even the survival, of stablecoins.

The paper’s clock starts at Circle’s message

The study follows Bitcoin and the five most liquid (most traded) stablecoins minute by minute from 4 to 18 March 2023, about 10,000 minutes a week, and compares the week before Circle’s message with the week after. Everything that follows rests on that comparison.

The week before held shocks of its own: Silvergate said it would close and SVB failed, and Bitcoin stood about 12% below its 4 March level by 10 March, still 7 to 9% below when Circle’s message came.

Deck 1 · The weekend · 5 of 5

Trading jumped in the coins in doubt, and dried up in one

The paper’s second view of the panic is trading volume. After Circle’s message, the paper reports, trading in USDC, DAI and TUSD surged: panic, in its reading, from holders afraid they could not get their money out. Bitcoin and Tether, the most liquid already, kept trading much as before.

The odd one out is the Binance dollar. Its volume fell away abruptly, which the paper reads as a preference for higher-quality coins; New York’s financial regulator had already ordered its issuer, Paxos, to stop issuing it.
