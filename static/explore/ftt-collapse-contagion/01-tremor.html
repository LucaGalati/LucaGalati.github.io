When an exchange’s own coin collapses, does the damage stay at home?

FTX, a crypto exchange based in the Bahamas, had issued a coin of its own, FTT, which also traded on other exchanges. At 05:59 UTC on 6 November 2022 came the first public report that FTX might be insolvent. Within a week it had stopped trading, and it went bankrupt; for about four days, the letter says, customers could still trade on it but withdraw almost nothing.

On FTX, a typical minute moved FTT’s price by about 0.10% in the week before the report and by about 2.37% in the week after, more than twenty times as much.

Did the collapse spread to the other exchanges where FTT traded? And inside FTX, where money could hardly leave, where did it go?

One coin, many price tags, tied together

Each exchange has its own price for FTT, set by its own buyers and sellers. When two prices drift apart, traders buy where the coin is cheap and sell where it is dear, which pulls them back together. So news that moves FTT on one exchange tends to move it on the others.

How closely two exchanges move is measured by a correlation: 0 when their minute-by-minute moves are unrelated, 1 when they move in lockstep, below 0 when they lean in opposite directions.

Minute by minute, much of each exchange’s movement is its own noise; what two exchanges share is the news. The more of it they share, the higher the correlation. In the collapse week, what would you expect the share to do?

Contagion, in this letter, means these ties tightening when a shock hits.

Every tie to FTX tightened, by more than chance would explain

The letter’s test takes FTX’s eight ties one at a time and sets the tie’s average in the week before against its average in the collapse week. It divides the gap by how uncertain the two averages are; the result is the t‑statistic. An average over many minutes is pinned down tightly: its uncertainty shrinks with the square root of the number of minutes.

The authors chose a strict bar on purpose: with so many minutes, a lenient one would flag almost any gap. The letter’s t‑statistics run from −19 to −47, and Table 2 marks all eight as significant. They read this as contagion across every exchange in the sample.

The tie jumped, sank back, and jumped again

Behind each weekly average is a path. Pick a venue: the line is its tie to FTX, minute by minute, traced from the letter’s Figure 2.

About ten hours after the report the ties jumped to between 0.7 and 0.9, sank back, and jumped again over the following days. In the collapse week the tie’s swings were four to ten times wider than in the week before.
