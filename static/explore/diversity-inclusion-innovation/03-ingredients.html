Inclusion’s link to innovation thins as women’s share at the top grows

Do the ingredients reinforce each other? The paper adds their product to the model. In the innovation equation it is negative: the more women in the executive team, the less extra innovation goes with each inclusion practice. The authors read it through fault lines: when a leadership team tilts far towards one group, subgroups can blunt what inclusive practices achieve, a case for balanced teams over maximal numbers (p. 14, 17). The chart turns the printed coefficients into inclusion’s link at each share.

Then switch to Tobin’s Q. On the straight road the same product is positive (5% level), so inclusion’s total link with market value grows with women’s share.

Most firms sit at the left of the chart: three firm-years in four have at most 22% women executives. The product is significant at the 1% level for patents and at the 10% level for environmental innovation. The lines are computed from the printed coefficients, without an uncertainty band.

Back to the question

Does diversity pay? Here, the share of women executives on its own shows no detectable overall link with value or profits once inclusion is counted. The D&I score does: compared with otherwise similar firms, those scoring higher were worth more and earned more two years later, and part of that went through what they invent.

The authors suggest this may be part of why earlier studies disagree: most looked only for a straight road, and only at diversity, leaving out both the detour and inclusion (p. 2, 17).

  1. The detour is notable but partial: about 11% of the link with Tobin’s Q and 22% of the link with return on assets run through patents, about 7.5% through environmental innovation.
  2. Inclusion practices carry most of it: per standard deviation, women executives go with less than a fifth of the extra innovation that inclusion goes with.
  3. The more women in the executive team, the weaker inclusion’s link to innovation, which the authors read as a case for balanced leadership. Inclusion’s total link with market value does not weaken.

Associations between comparable firms, not proof of cause: successful firms may be the ones that invest in D&I, inclusion is read from disclosed policies, and diversity from executives’ gender alone (Section 6.2).

Open questions the authors name: would the channel look the same with employees’ own experience of inclusion, with ethnic, cultural or age diversity, or in countries with board quotas?

To take with you: two firms with the same D&I score, one built from women executives, the other from inclusion practices. Which would this paper expect to file more patents, and why?
