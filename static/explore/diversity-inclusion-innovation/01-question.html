Does diversity pay? The research cannot agree.

Large firms spent three decades building diversity and inclusion (D&I) programmes. Lately many are taking them apart: close to 30% of the S&P 100 have formally scaled back their commitments.

If D&I paid, would they? The research gives no clear answer. A few of the studies this paper cites:

Found a gain

  • Women on the board, higher firm value Campbell & Mínguez-Vera 2008
  • Staff-rated D&I, higher later profits and value Edmans et al. 2023

Found a cost

  • Norway’s board quota, lower short-term profits Matsa & Miller 2013
  • Culturally diverse boards, lower value and profits Frijns et al. 2016

Found both

  • Women directors in France, higher profits but lower market value Bennouri et al. 2018

Found nothing

  • A demographically diverse workforce, no clear link Frijns et al. 2025

This paper suspects they watched only the straight road from D&I to performance. Perhaps much of its value takes a detour, through what a firm invents.

Two ingredients, one score

Diversity is the share of women among a firm’s executives: the chief executive, the finance chief and the other officers who decide where money and research go.

Inclusion counts how many of five workplace practices the firm reports, divided by five.

The D&I score is the average of the two, between 0 and 1. Build a firm and see where it lands against the paper’s 47,212 firm-years (a firm-year is one firm observed in one year).

Try reaching 0.25 without a single practice.

Place a bet: does a higher score go with a more valuable firm?

Measure a firm’s value with Tobin’s Q: the market value of its shares plus its debts, divided by the book value of its assets (sample average 1.78). Measure its profitability with return on assets (ROA): earnings as a share of assets (average 5.8%).

Take two firms alike in size, debt, growth, R&D, cash flow and board, once differences between years, sectors and countries are set aside, but whose D&I scores differ by one standard deviation, 0.14. Two years later, how does the higher-scoring firm’s value compare?

One standard deviation is a typical gap between two firms in the sample. For the D&I score it is 0.14, about the distance from the average score, 0.24, to 0.38.

Appendix A for the definitions. Table 3: Tobin’s Q mean 1.782; ROA mean 0.058; D&I mean 0.240, standard deviation 0.138.

Table 5, columns 1 and 3: D&I coefficients 0.875 (Tobin’s Q) and 0.027 (ROA), both at the 1% level, times 0.138, computed; the paper gives the magnitudes on p. 8. Intervals: coefficient ± 1.96 × coefficient ÷ t-statistic (6.86 and 4.18), computed. With sector-by-year and country-by-year effects (columns 2 and 4): 0.891 and 0.026.

Clearing the 1% bar. If there were no link at all, a gap this large would turn up by chance in fewer than one sample in a hundred. At the 5% or 10% level the evidence is weaker; ‘not significant’ means the estimate cannot be told apart from zero. Here both 95% intervals stay clear of zero.
