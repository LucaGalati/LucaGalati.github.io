Where the gaps sit, and how big they are

A pile of daily gaps can be summed up two ways: where they sit on average, and how big they typically are. The paper’s figures follow the typical size, year by year; its t-tests ask whether the average gap shifts when the window grows.

The two can tell different stories. In this toy the gaps swing by about 20 pips from day to day under either window, so their typical size hardly moves. A test on the differences looks at each day under both windows: the day’s own swing drops out, and the shift is judged against the small change from one window to the other. With enough days, a tenth of a pip clears the bar.

Significant means unlikely to be chance, not large. The authors describe the representativeness shift they find for EUR/USD and GBP/USD at twenty minutes as slight, not enough to warrant a change of method.

What the design can and cannot tell

  • No experimentThe window did not change during the sample, so the ten- and twenty-minute fixes are rebuilt from trading that took place under the five-minute rule. How traders would behave under a longer window is not observed.
  • A wide yardstickThe day’s average price can sit far from the price at 4 pm, so the representativeness gap is wide and varies a lot from one day to the next.
  • Few month-endsAbout one day in twenty, 214 per pair, so month-end differences rest on far fewer observations than those for normal days.
  • Mixed signsSeveral differences change sign from one pair to another; the authors draw their conclusions from the pattern across all six pairs.
  • The cost of changeA longer window would mean re-assessing the surveillance algorithms that look for manipulation and moving resources; that cost lies outside what the data measure.
  • Market, not methodThe data show how the market has affected the fix, not whether the method itself kept market impact down; the authors reworded one research question for that reason.
  • Reversals, not causesA test of whether moves into the fix are undone afterwards finds no significant link in most quarters, the exceptions mainly in 2020 and 2021; like the earlier study it follows, the paper does not seek a causal explanation.
