Why does the 4 pm fix last five minutes?

The currency market never closes: about $7.5 trillion a day, traded around the clock, mostly directly between two parties and spread over many platforms. So it has no closing price. The WM/Refinitiv 4 pm fix stands in: one reference rate per currency pair, computed from a short window of trading around 4 pm in London. Fund managers rebalance at it, companies value their foreign-currency holdings with it, and multi-currency stock, bond and credit indices are built on it, so large sums hang on those few minutes.

In 2013 traders were found to have exploited the way it was computed, often at their clients’ expense; five banks were fined £1.1 billion. In 2015 the window was stretched from one minute to five. Some now argue for twenty.

If one minute was too short, would twenty be better?

A fix is an average over a window

In this toy, the fix is simply the average of the prices seen in the window around 4 pm. Now someone buys hard for one minute at four o’clock, lifting the price for a moment before it falls back.

Stretch the window. The same burst is shared among more prices, so it moves the fix less, measured in pips: the fourth decimal of most rates, as in 1.2650 to 1.2651. That is the logic behind lengthening it in 2015.

So is longer simply better? A longer fix also averages prices further from four o’clock. The paper asks what that costs.

Toy: an invented price path and an invented five-pip burst, to show the mechanism; these are not the paper’s data. The real recipe samples prices from three trading platforms and checks each one before computing the rate (p. 2 and p. 4).

Three yardsticks

The paper borrows its yardsticks from a study for the UK regulator (Evans et al., 2018). Each is a daily gap, in pips, between the fix and something it should be close to:

  • RepresentativenessDoes it reflect that day’s market? The gap to the day’s average traded price.
  • AttainabilityCan a bank that promised a client the fix get it? The gap to the average price traded in the window.
  • RobustnessDoes it hold up against manipulation and bad data? The gap to the same fix without its outlier checks.

Over each year, the daily gaps are summed up as a root mean square: the typical size of the gap.

Nine years, six currency pairs, three windows

The authors had Refinitiv’s own record of every order, cancellation and trade, to the millisecond, on the three platforms the fix is computed from: Refinitiv Matching, EBS and Currenex. It runs from 15 February 2015, when the five-minute method took effect, to the end of 2023.

With it they rebuilt the fix every day over five, ten and twenty minutes, the same way each time, and each once more without the outlier checks. Every yardstick then has three values a day, and a simple question: do the longer windows score better?
