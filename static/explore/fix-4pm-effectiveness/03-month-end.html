Month-end blurs the picture

On the last trading day of each month funds rebalance, and earlier work found trading spikes around the fix on those days. The authors note it is when the largest share of volume goes through the platforms. That is about one trading day in twenty: 214 per pair in the sample.

Switch between normal and month-end days. Each dot is one test of whether the average daily gap shifts when the window grows; filled where the paper marks the shift significant, more solidly for the stricter levels.

On normal days nearly every matching and unchecked-fix test is significant, but only two of the twelve representativeness tests are; on month-end days fewer than half as many tests are significant.

At month-end, the half hour before counts

The regression the authors committed to in advance slices the half hour before the window into five-minute slots. For each it asks: do trading costs there line up with the level of that day’s fix, and differently at month-end?

  • Bid–ask spreadthe gap between the best price to buy and the best price to sell
  • Effective spreadwhat traders paid against the midpoint between those two prices
  • Price impacthow far that midpoint moved in the 20 seconds after a trade

The authors set the signs aside. That conditions before the window line up with the fix at month-end is, for them, a sign it “may be sub-optimal” on those days, though not by itself a sign that the method is failing.

What to take away

Nine years, six pairs: a longer window would have changed little, and not all of it for the better.

  1. A longer window dilutes any one burst of trading, but it averages over more of the afternoon; the paper weighs that with three yardsticks.
  2. Ten or twenty minutes barely change how well the fix reflects the day’s market, with small exceptions for EUR/USD and GBP/USD.
  3. In the authors’ reading they make the fix harder to match and slightly more robust. With the cost of change, five minutes “remains broadly effective”.
  4. Month-end is different: far fewer window differences are significant, and trading costs before the window line up with the fix. The authors call it possibly “sub-optimal” then, without reading this as the method failing.

The longer fixes are rebuilt from trading under the five-minute rule, so how traders would react to a longer window is not observed. Several of the differences change sign from one pair to another.

What the design can and cannot tell →

Among the authors’ ideas for future work: change the average itself, a trimmed or winsorized mean or the Hodges–Lehmann estimator, rather than the window.
