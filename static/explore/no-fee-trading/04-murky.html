Murky

What the exchange got.

Binance gave up its fee, its main source of revenue, on thirteen pairs. In return, on Bitcoin–Tether: 3.8 times as many trades a minute and 2.8 times the volume, with an all-time high on launch day. Trading in Ether on Binance rose too, though nothing had changed for it.

The average trade shrank by a third. The paper reads this as who arrived: smaller, likely retail traders, drawn by the fee they could see; the paper argues they missed the spread they could not. Citing Kaiko, it reports Binance’s market share surging past its rivals’ by more than 20%, and reverting once the programme was halted.

→ Why give away your main revenue?

Why give it away

What zero fees bought the exchange.

The paper offers the explanation it can support: a customer-attraction strategy, launched after the stablecoin collapse of May 2022 and extended, perhaps, as FTX neared insolvency. Market share rose while the fee was zero and reverted when it came back; the paper calls it “a customer attraction strategy that went wrong”. Binance explained neither the choice of the thirteen pairs nor the extension.

Then it names the question it cannot answer. Zero fees created “a favorable environment for market makers”, whose quoting revenues rose; the exchange’s own gain was market share. Whether the exchange and the market-making firms were connected, the paper does not know. It says so plainly, it has no evidence of collusion, and it leaves the question to future work.

The word in the title is murky, not guilty.

Context the paper gives, not evidence: in 2023 the SEC filed thirteen charges against Binance and its chief executive, on other matters; the paper notes the exchange appears to have settled.

The scorecard

What the theory got wrong.

The paper drew its hypotheses from the standard model of a limit-order market in which fees are paid straight to the exchange. The predictions about how much people would trade held: more quotes, more trades, more fills, and more volume against other exchanges.

The predictions about the price of trading and the quantity on offer failed, and so did the one about trade size. This was the cleanest setting the models ask for: no broker in between, a tick size close to nothing. The paper’s conclusion is that mainstream theory “might need to be reexamined” for crypto-asset markets.

→ Was any rule broken?

Rules

There was no rule for this to break.

Crypto exchanges in 2022 were self-regulated. In a more regulated market, the paper notes, such behaviour “might already be scrutinized under fair trading practices”. Here it can be called “potentially unethical”, the paper’s phrase, or read the other way, as “a strategic adaptation to changing market conditions”.

Its suggestions: isolate market makers from the influence of the exchanges they trade on; treat an exchange’s unilateral fee-setting as a competition question; and build computerised surveillance that can see what this paper saw, in line with IOSCO’s 2023 recommendations.

→ What is left to ask

Open questions

What you can now say in one breath.

Zero fees brought nearly four times the trades and smaller traders; market makers widened the quoted spread six-fold and kept the saving the fee cut created, so what takers paid in spread did not fall, while the exchange’s market share rose.

What the paper leaves open:

  • What happened when the fees came back? Binance later ended the programme; the paper names the reintroduction as the next experiment.
  • Were the makers and the exchange connected? The paper has no evidence either way.
  • Do retail traders notice the second bill? The paper calls the apparent discount a cognitive bias and leaves it for future research.
