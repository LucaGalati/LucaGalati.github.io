Free · The gift

An explorable explanation

If trading is free, what does it cost?

On 8 July 2022, at two in the afternoon UTC, the largest cryptocurrency exchange in the world stopped charging its customers to trade Bitcoin.

Binance was turning five. For thirteen Bitcoin pairs the trading fee, 0.10% a side for an ordinary customer, went to zero. It was meant to be a six-month trial.

But a fee is only one price of trading. The other, the spread that market makers set, is what a paper measured minute by minute. Theory said it should fall too.

Two bills

Every trade pays twice.

Every trade has two sides. The maker posts an order and waits; the taker hits an order that is already there.

The first bill goes to the exchange: a fee, a slice of the amount traded. An ordinary Binance customer paid 0.10% each way, or 10 basis points (hundredths of a percent).

The second goes to whoever posted the order you hit: the market maker, who sells a little above the mid-price and buys a little below it. That gap is the bid–ask spread. The first bill is printed on a fee schedule; the second is set minute by minute by the people who make the market.

→ Now remove the first bill

Place your bet

On 8 July the first bill disappeared.

Makers and takers now paid nothing to the exchange. Standard theory (Colliard and Foucault, 2012) says what follows: a cut in the exchange’s total fee is passed on, makers compete, and the spread tightens.

Where did the gap on screen go in the week after, on Bitcoin–Tether at Binance, the most liquid pair in the market? Drag the gold ring to your guess.

Bitcoin–Tether on Binance went from 0.29 to 1.70 basis points between the best bid and the best ask: nearly six times wider. The same pair narrowed on FTX and barely moved on Kucoin; Ether on Binance, still paying the fee, held still.
