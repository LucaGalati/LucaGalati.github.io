The experiment

Before and after is not enough.

July 2022 was not a quiet month. Stablecoins had collapsed in May, and FTX, one of the exchanges in this story, would be insolvent within months. Anything measured on Binance that week might have happened anyway.

So the paper sets the treated pair beside three that shared its weather but kept their fees: the same pair, Bitcoin–Tether, on FTX and Kucoin, the third- and fifth-largest exchanges; and Ether–Tether on Binance itself.

If zero fees did something, it should show on Binance’s Bitcoin–Tether and nowhere else. The treated pair’s change minus a control’s change is the estimate: a difference-in-differences. Pick a control.

Each control has its quirks, FTX’s coming collapse among them; that is one reason for three controls, not one. The paper repeats the test on three smaller zero-fee pairs: BTCEUR, BTCGBP and BTCUSDC.

→ What was measured

Two weeks of ticks

Every trade and every quote, the switch exactly in the middle.

For each of the four markets the paper has every trade and every change in the best bid and offer, stamped to the millisecond, with the side that started each trade (Refinitiv Tick History). Crypto never closes, so the sample runs 24 hours a day: one week before 8 July 14:00 UTC and one week after, averaged into 20,160 one-minute intervals per market.

In the paper’s hourly charts the treated pair’s measures jump at the switch instead of drifting into it — “every adjustment was instantaneous” — which points to fast traders reacting at once.

What the regression adds

Averages can be moved by other things.

Price, volatility, turnover and how concentrated trading is across exchanges all shift from week to week. The paper’s main test is a panel regression with those as controls. The number that matters is the coefficient on treated × after: the extra change on the zero-fee pairs after 8 July, beyond the controls’ change.

Pooled across the four zero-fee pairs it could observe, the quoted spread rose 3.6 bps — more than Bitcoin–Tether’s 1.4, because the three smaller pairs, quoted several times wider throughout (sample means of 3.7 to 7.6 bps against 1.0), widened more: about 5.5 bps against the euro, 4.5 against sterling and 2.4 against USDC.

→ What this design cannot rule out

Limits

What this design cannot rule out.

  • Only a week on each side. The paper reports similar results over a month around the event, but does not tabulate them (footnote 13).
  • Binance chose the thirteen pairs; nobody drew them at random. The paper calls the group “arguably” exogenous (Section 3).
  • One control was itself touched. Ether on Binance kept its fee, yet its trades rose 41% as traders moved to the exchange (Table 2), which blurs comparisons against it.
  • Behaviour the models do not cover. The paper allows that the switch “may result in irrational changes in traders’ behavior” it cannot explain.

Within those limits, the pattern holds against each of the three controls taken one at a time: the gap on screen widened, depth fell, and the makers’ take rose by about 1.8 to 2.6 bps (Table B.1).
