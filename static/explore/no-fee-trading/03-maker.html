The maker’s quote

Two spreads: the gap on screen, and what a trade paid.

The quoted spread is the gap between the best bid and the best ask, averaged each minute: what the screen shows. The effective spread compares each trade’s price with the mid-price at that moment, doubled for a round trip: what a trade actually paid.

They are not the same. In the week before 8 July the screen showed 0.29 bps while trades paid 1.80; the paper does not break down the gap.

In the week after, the screen widened nearly six-fold, to 1.70. What trades paid rose by about a tenth, to 1.98 — and by about as much on FTX (+0.23) and Kucoin (+0.16), where nothing had changed. For the taker, the spread bill did not fall.

→ What does the maker earn?

What a maker earns

The maker does not keep the spread.

Round trip, the taker pays the maker the effective spread: 1.80 bps on Bitcoin–Tether at Binance in the week before 8 July. But some takers know something. In the 20 seconds after a trade the mid-price tends to move against the maker: that price impact was 3.67 bps.

What is left is the realized spread, the maker’s earnings: 1.80 − 3.67 ≈ −1.86 bps (unrounded, 1.805 − 3.669). By this measure making the market lost money on the average round trip, as it did that week on every control market.

Why quote at a loss on average? The paper does not say; what it tests is the change, and that is what the fee moved.

→ Then the fee vanished

The saving

Removing the fee cut the maker’s cost.

Price impact fell from 3.67 to 1.47 bps. The paper’s reading: quotes no longer had to build the fee into the price, and a zero commission does not distinguish between orders that make liquidity and orders that take it, so traders choose by the posted spread alone.

On Bitcoin–Tether the drop holds against every control (Table B.1: −1.8 to −2.7 bps); pooled with the three smaller pairs, where price impact barely moved, it is not significant (Table 3).

The paper’s Hypothesis 3, from the standard model: with the fee gone, what takers pay should fall. A drop in the maker’s own costs is exactly what competition would pass on.

→ Set the quote yourself

Set the quote

You are the market maker.

You post a bid and an ask. A taker crosses them and pays you; then the price drifts against you as informed traders reveal what they knew. You keep the difference.

Not in this ledger: the fee itself. Until 8 July you also paid the exchange on every fill, from 0.10% a side for an ordinary account down to 0.012% at the top tier; afterwards, nothing. Mid-price: the sample mean (Table 1).

Competition

Why didn’t a rival undercut them?

The makers kept the saving and offered less, too: the typical quantity at the best prices fell to about a sixth while it rose on every control, in the paper’s words “liquidity deterioration for takers”. A rival quoting a little tighter should have taken the business. Why none did, the paper does not test. It offers three readings, with very different evidence behind them:

  • the paper’s reading Customers saw the fee, not the spread: trades shrank by a third, a sign of retail traders drawn by an apparent discount.
  • the paper’s concern One venue pulled the trade from its rivals, creating “a favorable environment for market makers”.
  • no evidence Ties between the exchange and the makers: the paper raises them and says it has no evidence.
