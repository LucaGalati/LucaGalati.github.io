    The dealer has a window problem too ›

    The dealer’s window

    The dealer is paid the fix. Part of what the fix captures is the dealer’s own fading impact, so a short window is a source of revenue. A longer window dilutes that revenue faster than it saves the dealer on execution.

    On top of that, matching a continuous average with 25 discrete trades leaves a tracking error that grows with the window. Both push its risk-adjusted profit down; past a break-even window it is negative.

    In GBP/USD, from one minute to ten: revenue −0.81 bp, execution cost saved +0.76, tracking charge −0.05. Drag the window and read the dealer’s book.

    Why does it trade before the window at all?

    Dealer’s split

    The fee

    A dealer facing competition does not work at a loss. It charges a fee equal to the shortfall, and the client pays it. Beyond the break-even window the client’s true cost bends upward, and its best window moves in.

    With the split held at a tenth, the fee is easy to see: 11.5 minutes become 9.6. Now let the dealer choose its own best split. It needs a far smaller fee, about 0.02 bp in a median GBP/USD week, and the client’s window barely moves: 11.9 to 11.5, the paper’s median-week answer (Table E.1).

    The fee is the channel by which the dealer’s risk reaches the client. In the paper’s weekly data it matters most in the more volatile AUD/USD and at longer windows (p. 34).

    The fix has other users ›

    Four objectives, four answers

    The client is not the only user of the fix. The paper scores four objectives in the same cost units: the client’s cost, the dealer’s profit, representativeness (how close the published rate stays to the untouched 4pm price) and attainability (how closely anyone can replicate the fix by trading).

    The dealer wants the shortest window: a short one brings it the most impact revenue and the least tracking risk. Attainability agrees. Representativeness sits left of the client: its risk weight is eight times larger and its variance grows four times more slowly. In stressed weeks those two move left: even the client wants about five minutes for GBP/USD and two for AUD/USD.

    One window for everyone

    An administrator cannot publish a different window for each user. The paper’s administrator minimises a weighted sum of the four losses — 35% client, 15% dealer, 35% representativeness, 15% attainability. This is the sandbox.

    Move the market first: it is the market, more than the weights, that moves the window. Watch the readout too: at the GBP/USD medians five minutes costs about 6% more than the best window; at the AUD/USD medians, almost nothing.

    The paper’s own numbers: across two years of weeks, the median of the weekly optimal windows is 8.56 minutes for GBP/USD and 4.47 for AUD/USD (Figure 9); at the median parameters it finds about 9 and 4.5 (Table E.1). This toy gives both currencies the same rounded impact parameters and lands near 9.2 and 5.8.

    But a benchmark cannot change every week ›

    Where five minutes sits

    A benchmark has to use the same window every week. So the paper asks which fixed window does best across all the weeks: in a typical week (lowest median loss) and in a stressed one (lowest 99th-percentile loss).

    Everyone’s fear of risk

    Five minutes is “not the exact optimum for every participant, currency, or market state”, the paper concludes, but it sits in a relatively robust region: inside the stressed range for GBP/USD and the typical range for AUD/USD, a few minutes from the rest. How close depends on everyone’s fear of risk, which the paper sets rather than estimates. Flip it.
