Trading before the window

Why buy before the window opens? The dealer is paid the fix, so what matters is how much of each purchase the fix will see. Three forces pull different ways.

  • Permanent impact. An early trade lifts every price the fix averages; one inside lifts only what comes after it. Early buying raises the fix: more revenue for the dealer, more cost for the client.
  • Fading impact. Early bumps have faded by the time the window opens, so less of them reaches the fix: cheaper for the client, less benchmark revenue for the dealer (what it earns because the fix includes its own impact).
  • Tracking. The dealer promised a continuous average but trades in lumps; a small early purchase makes its average follow the fix more closely. As the window grows, this force is the one that remains.

For the client and the administrator the response is a rounding error: more transient impact still means a longer window (+2.9 minutes for GBP/USD), more volatility a shorter one (−2.5). For the dealer it is the story: higher volatility makes it pre-hedge less, leaves more of the order inside the window, and reverses the sign. The design conclusions survive.

Centre the window

Remember the gap between the fix and the 4:00 price? Representativeness asks how big it tends to be: does the published 4pm rate stay close to the untouched 4pm price? The variance of the gap is σ²Tf(α² − α + 1/3), where α says where “4pm” sits inside the window (eq. 67). Slide it.

At either edge the factor is 1/3; at the centre it is 1/12, a quarter of the variance. WMR centres its window on 4pm, and the paper adopts the same convention. A longer window still widens the gap: that is the cost representativeness counts.

Attainability

Attainability is how closely anyone, whether a corporate treasurer, a fund or another dealer, can replicate the published fix by trading. In the model it is the tracking error of a replicator who trades like the dealer but picks its own early share.

Even if the replicator re-optimises its early trading for every window, each extra minute still adds tracking error (eq. 87). So attainability always prefers the shortest window.

But it is small. Its weight pulls the administrator’s answer toward shorter windows, though only slightly: in the toy, dropping it moves the answer by three seconds, from 9.15 to 9.20 minutes. Most of the gap between the client’s eleven minutes and the administrator’s nine comes from representativeness.

What the numbers rest on

  • Data. LSEG Matching trades (direction, volume) and best quotes, GBP/USD and AUD/USD, 1 July 2024 – 26 June 2026.
  • Impact, weekly, 3:52:30–4:02:30pm London: a Hasbrouck (1991) autoregression of one-second returns on signed volume, net of the order flow a trade predicts (Eisler, Bouchaud and Kockelkoren’s “bare” impact), fitted to a lasting step plus a fading bump.
  • Volatility from five-second returns over 3:30–3:45pm, before fixing flow dominates.
  • Medians: 0.01 bp per million for good, 0.06–0.07 that fades; half-lives near 51 s (GBP/USD) and 58 s (AUD/USD); typical one-minute moves of 1.60 and 2.35 bp.
  • Risk aversion is set, not estimated: a basis point of risk is worth about a third of a basis point of expected cost to the client (γC = 0.0005), three-quarters to the dealer, a half to representativeness.

Where the model stops

Left out

  • Manipulation. Resistance to it depends mostly on how trades are weighted and trimmed inside the window, which the paper does not model.
  • Market power. Competing dealers set fees at break-even; with market power the client would pay more.
  • Changing volatility. Scheduled news near 4pm could change where the window should sit.
  • Free trade timing. Letting the dealer time each trade changes little, except when permanent impact is large next to transient impact (Appendix E).

Checked

  • A median fix instead of a mean: little change (Appendix D).
  • Order size. Larger orders want longer windows, with a diminishing effect: 6.2, 10.7 and 11.3 minutes for 10, 50 and 200 million (GBP/USD client, Table B.2).
  • Who picks the split. Letting the client choose it moves the GBP/USD answer from 10.7 to 10.5 minutes (Appendix C).
  • Risk aversion. The trade-offs survive, but the numbers move: the GBP/USD administrator’s window is about 13.5 minutes with less risk-averse users and 4.8 with more risk-averse ones (Table B.1).

The paper’s conclusion is a modest one: five minutes is not the exact optimum for every participant, currency or market state, but a practical compromise across them. EUR/USD and other benchmarks are left for future work.
