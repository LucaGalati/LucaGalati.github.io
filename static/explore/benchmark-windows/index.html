Limits to Longer Benchmark Windows

At four o’clock in London, a number is born: the WMR 4pm fix, the average of the prices traded in a short window around four. Funds holding about $6 trillion convert their currencies at it.

After the manipulation scandals (£1.1 billion of FCA fines in a single day), the window was widened in 2015 from one minute to five. Providers now sell twenty-minute versions.

If one minute was too short, why not twenty? Why not an hour?

One minute was easy to push around. This paper sets manipulation aside and asks a different question: what does a longer window cost the people who trade at the fix, and everyone who relies on it?

An average over a window

A fixing is not a price anyone quoted. It is the average of the prices traded inside a window, and the WMR window is centred on 4pm: five minutes run from 3:57:30 to 4:02:30. The bar drawn on gold is the fix. Drag the window.

The dealer hedges

A pension fund wants to buy £50 million “at the fix”. A dealer promises it the fix price, whatever that turns out to be, and buys the pounds in the market. In the paper’s model it makes 25 equal trades in the ten minutes before the window and 25 inside it. Say a tenth of the order goes before: an illustrative split; a dealer optimising for itself pre-hedges less.

With the market’s own movement switched off, watch only the dealer. Each trade nudges the price up, so the fix averages a price the dealer itself is lifting — and the pension fund pays every basis point of it.

Impact fades

Most of a trade’s footprint is temporary. In GBP/USD each million traded leaves about 0.01 basis points for good and 0.06–0.07 that fade, halving every 51 seconds or so: the paper’s median estimates from two years of trades around the fix.

Click the timeline to fire trades of 2 million; 25 of them make the whole order. Close together, the bumps stack. Spread out, each fades before the next arrives.

So a longer window spreads the dealer’s trades thinner and lets each bump decay before it is averaged. The paper calls this transient-impact dilution.

Same 25 trades: about 1.5 bp of average displacement packed into a minute, about 0.6 bp spread over ten.

So: longer is better?

For a client that cares only about the expected cost of its order, yes. Drag the window out to an hour.

The curve flattens but never turns up. Absent fees or an outside limit, a client that ignores risk prefers the longest possible window (Section 3.1). Yet the industry settled on five minutes. Something is missing from this picture.
